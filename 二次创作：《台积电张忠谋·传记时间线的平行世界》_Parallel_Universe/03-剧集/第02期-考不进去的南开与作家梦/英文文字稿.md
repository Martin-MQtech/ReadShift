# Morris Chang's Parallel World · Episode 02: "The School He Couldn't Get Into, and the Dream That Couldn't Feed Him"

> AIGC Parallel Biography — "The Parallel World of the TSMC Founder's Timeline"
> This episode's lens: **How does a boy with no connections open every door the era hands him?** From Chongqing to Shanghai — two sentences, two turning points, one coming-of-age.
> Target length: 20 minutes (English ~3,300 words)
> Original creative work based on public record; no text from any copyrighted book is reproduced.

## Opening Hook

[SFX: the clatter of mahjong tiles, women's laughter, fading to a hush]

Chongqing, 1943. In a living room, a group of ladies are playing mahjong.

A boy stands near the doorway, out of sight, and overhears his mother talking with the wife of a government official.

The lady asks about the boy's schooling. His mother says he wants to sit the entrance exam for the best middle school in Chongqing — Nankai.

The lady sets down her tiles, glances at his mother, and says it plainly:

"He won't get in. You'd better start pulling strings, calling in favors."

[Main narrator]

Those words hit him like a bucket of cold water.

The boy knew exactly what his family was: refugees from out of town, no roots in Chongqing, no connections to call on, no favors owed.

Standing there by the door, his heart sank.

But there was another sentence waiting for him in the years ahead — one that would land on him just as softly. Shanghai, 1948. He was seventeen, and he had just told his father he wanted to be a writer. His father set down his chopsticks, almost smiling, and said:

"Writing is a difficult way to make a living. If you become a writer, you may go hungry."

[Main narrator]

"Won't get in." "May go hungry."

Two sentences, heard by the same boy, in the same storm of an era.

The first one forced him to fight his way through a door. The second quietly closed the door on a dream.

This is the story of those two sentences — and of a boy who, in a world turned upside down, learned to open doors with nothing but his own hands, and to let one window close without letting its light go out.

---

## Act One · The Problem in the Mountain City

[Main narrator]

In 1943, Chongqing was China's wartime capital — the great rear base of a nation at war.

Into a mountain city of barely four hundred thousand people poured thousands of government offices, factories, schools, and refugees. Around Shapingba, schools clustered so thickly that the district became known as the wartime "cultural zone." And the most famous school of them all was Nankai.

Nankai had been founded by Zhang Boling, one of China's greatest educators. Before the war he had built his schools in Tianjin. When the city fell, Nankai University was forced to move west and join Peking University and Tsinghua in Kunming to form the celebrated National Southwestern Associated University. Meanwhile Zhang Boling himself came to Chongqing, and in 1936 opened a new Nankai middle school at Shapingba.

How hard was it to get in?

Every year, the entrance exam had a fixed number of places — and a crush of applicants. A refugee boy, a stranger in town, with no background and no connections, dreaming of Nankai: by the logic of the era, it was almost absurd.

The official's wife wasn't being cruel. She was being realistic.

[Main narrator, slower]

But the boy's mother did not give up.

She had heard that every summer, Nankai ran a summer school — and that the best students could be admitted directly, without sitting the exam.

So she made a decision: send the boy to the summer school.

It would brush up his studies. And — it was a gamble.

---

## Act Two · The Summer School and the Comeback

[Main narrator]

The boy knew this was his only chance.

In occupied Hong Kong, he had managed only half a semester of middle school. The chaos of war had swallowed most of a year. Now he would have to enter the second year of middle school as a transfer student — competing with boys who had never stopped studying. On paper, he was behind.

But the boy had something the others didn't: he genuinely wanted to learn.

At the summer school, he threw himself into it. Lessons by day, review by night, clawing back every subject he had lost.

[Main narrator]

And then came the result that surprised even him.

When the summer school ended, his grades were extraordinary. The school admitted him directly — as a full, regular student.

Word reached home, and his parents were overjoyed. His mother especially: in a culture where face mattered, she could finally hold her head up among her mahjong friends.

The official's wife never knew it, but her careless sentence had become the boy's best motivation.

[Main narrator]

And looking back, it reads almost like a prophecy of his whole life:

In this world, a boy with no connections opens doors with ability.

He pushed open the door of Nankai with his grades. Decades later, he would push open other doors — including one no one in the world had ever seen before.

---

## Act Three · Days and Nights at Shapingba

[Main narrator]

Life at Nankai was not easy.

The school required all students to board. The dormitory was one long hall, lined with double-decker wooden beds. Eight beds formed a cubicle; between cubicle and corridor there wasn't even a partition.

The dormitory's most famous residents were bedbugs. Fresh bedding became infested within days. The first nights were sleepless; in time, you simply got used to it — a few red marks by morning, and by night you slept like a log.

The food was worse. Wartime Chongqing meant coarse yellow rice, meals mostly vegetarian, with a piece of red-braised pork appearing maybe once a week at the famous "meat day." Sichuan pickled vegetables were plentiful, almost every meal. When the food ran out before the hunger did, boys would spoon soy sauce over their rice and eat it that way.

[Main narrator]

But hard as it was, the boy was thrilled.

It was his first time boarding, his first taste of communal life, his first time outside his parents' care. The school ran on a strict clock: reveille at six, classes at eight, mandatory evening study from seven to nine, lights out at nine-thirty. Before exams, daring students would sneak candles for all-nighters — strictly forbidden, punished with demerits if caught.

Years later he would say that the happiest, fastest, most friendship-filled years of his education were the ones he spent in that dormitory.

[Main narrator]

And it was here, too, that a seed was planted — the seed of literature.

As a child, the boy had been sickly, no good at sports, often alone at home with a set of children's encyclopedias called the Children's Library. At Nankai, his Chinese teacher guided him into classical prose and modern literature. He wrote for wall newspapers, submitted pieces, and the more he wrote, the more he loved it.

In his third year, he and some classmates started a wall newspaper called the "Jian Bao" — from the ancient Book of Changes: "As Heaven maintains vigor through movement, a gentleman should constantly strive for self-improvement." He ran the literature page and wrote an essay every week.

To launch the paper, he twice went to call on the school's principal, Zhang Boling.

The old man was in his sixties, the founder of the Nankai schools, chairman of the National Political Council — a man before whom even Chiang Kai-shek showed deference. Yet to a teenager, he showed no airs at all. He taught gently, patiently, like a grandfather.

## Act Four · The Literary Boy of the Shanghai Bund

[SFX: the horn of a Huangpu River ferry; a newsboy's cry in the distance, fading]

[Main narrator]

In 1947, the boy moved with his family to Shanghai.

The war was changing; so was his family's address. Midway through the school year, he entered the attached middle school of St. John's University — a leafy campus behind Jessfield Park. Half a year later, he sat the exam for Nanyang Model High School, entering the second year as a transfer student.

Nanyang Model was one of Shanghai's finest schools, famous above all for mathematics and science. Most of its graduates went on to Jiaotong University — a school so strong in engineering it was called "China's MIT." People joked that Nanyang Model was really "Jiaotong's prep school," and the school didn't mind at all.

But the boy inside this temple of mathematics carried a head full of literature.

He wrestled, half-understanding, through Laozi, Han Feizi, Xunzi, the Book of Songs, the Records of the Grand Historian — and devoured modern writers: Lu Xun, Hu Shih, Xu Zhimo, Mao Dun, Ba Jin, Bing Xin, Cao Yu... and Xu Xu.

Xu Xu was his favorite. He loved the "light melancholy, the faint sorrow" of his fiction.

That year, a novel called "Wind and Rain" had recently appeared. The boy read it at least three times.

[Main narrator, slower]

A seventeen-year-old, in a school famous for math, secretly in love with a novelist known for atmosphere, rereading one book three times — literature had grown into a tree inside him.

He had read a few books now, published a few essays for middle-school readers, and he began to think, seriously: could he become a writer?

Looking back, it was a naive wish — he had read so little. But to the boy, it was as sincere as a wish can be.

[Main narrator, dialogue]

Fortunately, his father woke him from the dream — quickly, and with skill.

The father knew perfectly well that his son had no idea how far a real writer stood from him. But he didn't say that. He didn't scold, didn't lecture. He simply remarked, worldly-wise, with a smile:

"Writing is a difficult way to make a living. If you become a writer, you may go hungry."

[Main narrator]

And the boy had absolute respect for his father's judgment.

His father was a Ningbo man who had spent his career in banking, who had seen too much. He knew that writing could not feed a family — not out of prejudice, but out of the hard schooling of his generation.

The boy did not argue, did not resist. He simply let the ambition fade.

---

## Act Five · The Golden Yuan: The Era's "You May Go Hungry"

[Main narrator]

How heavy those words were, the boy soon saw with his own eyes.

In the second half of 1948, the war situation collapsed. Hyperinflation had been poisoning the economy for years; now, as the war turned, prices climbed to desperate heights.

That summer, the government reformed the currency, replacing the fabi with the "gold yuan" notes, and forbidding people from holding gold or dollars. Everyone's gold and foreign currency had to be exchanged for the new notes within a strict deadline.

Nobody trusted the gold yuan. Money could become wastepaper overnight — so what do you do? You buy things. Shanghai poured into real estate, and property prices doubled, tripled, in a single breath.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

The frenzy lasted barely two months.

The war worsened. Armies pressed toward Xuzhou; the decisive Huaihai Campaign was about to begin. Panic seized Shanghai. Property prices, which had soared overnight, crashed below their original levels just as fast.

The irony was a tragedy that ruined countless families.

[Main narrator, quiet]

Now look back at that dinner table.

The father said "you may go hungry" on the eve of that madness.

A banker who had spent a lifetime watching his colleagues, his friends, his neighbors go bankrupt in inflation — no one knew better what a young man who lived by his pen would face in such an age.

"May go hungry" was not conservatism. It was not discouragement. It was the blood-and-tears wisdom an era had taught a father.

## Act Six · Years Later, Two Doors Both Opened

[Main narrator, with feeling, slower]

The boy left Shanghai for Hong Kong, and from there boarded a ship for America.

He became an engineer, then a manager, and finally — past fifty — he returned to Taiwan and founded a company the world had never seen before.

Years later, the boy was an old man, running the largest semiconductor company on earth.

One day, drafting the company's operating philosophy, he struggled for days and produced ten principles, proud of what he thought was a lifetime's distilled wisdom. Then he happened to see, again, the eight characters of the Nankai motto — "Yun Gong Yun Neng, Ri Xin Yue Yi": be public-spirited, be capable; renew yourself every day.

He was stunned.

His ten proud principles, in the end, had not gone beyond those eight characters.

[Main narrator]

And the dream that had been pressed down?

The old man wrote later that his boyhood dream of being a writer had become only a memory. He asked himself: can I still write long-form Chinese?

And then he spent two years, and hundreds of hours, writing — word by word — an autobiography of several hundred thousand characters.

The dream that had been pressed down at the dinner table woke up on its own, after he passed sixty.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

One more thing.

Decades later, in a small town in central Taiwan, he happened to pass a secondhand bookstore — and there on the shelf, he saw a copy of "Wind and Rain."

He bought it at once, carried it back to his hotel, and stayed up the night rereading it, savoring the taste of his boyhood.

Xu Xu had long since died. His reputation had faded after his death. The old man still believed, to this day, that Xu Xu should be remembered as a far greater writer than he was.

## Epilogue · A Parallel View

[Main narrator, quiet, slower]

Let us go back to those two sentences.

"Won't get in." — In the official's wife's world, doors open with connections.

"May go hungry." — In the father's world, you choose the safe road.

The boy answered both with his life.

The lady was half wrong. Doors can be opened with ability — he pushed open the door of Nankai with his grades in that summer school. Decades later, he pushed open a door no one in the world had ever seen, with the same strength.

The father was right, and wrong. Writing indeed could not feed a family — the boy never became a writer. But the dream never really went out: it became the eloquence of his speeches, the depth of his autobiography, an old man's lifelong devotion to Chinese letters.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

When he founded TSMC, he gave the company four core values: Integrity, Commitment, Innovation, and Customer Trust — known, in short, as "ICIC."

The "Gong" of Nankai — public-spiritedness — became integrity, responsibility, a promise to customers and society. The "Xin" — renewal — became innovation, never standing still.

What a refugee boy learned in a Nankai dormitory, eating rice with soy sauce, grew at last into a company that changed the world.

This was the boy's coming-of-age:

**Connections may hand you a ticket, but only ability keeps you in your seat. And the dream that was pressed down — it is what teaches you to remember who you are.**

## Knowledge Card · Historical Background of This Episode

(Verified public facts, for the listener's reference)

1. **Nankai Middle School, Chongqing**: Founded in 1936 by Zhang Boling at Shapingba, part of the Nankai school system. During the war, Shapingba became a famous "cultural zone" of schools.
2. **Zhang Boling (1876–1951)**: Founder of the Nankai schools. During the war, Nankai University joined Peking University and Tsinghua in Kunming to form the National Southwestern Associated University. Nankai's motto: "Yun Gong Yun Neng, Ri Xin Yue Yi."
3. **Nanyang Model High School**: A prestigious Shanghai school tracing its roots to 1901, closely linked to Nanyang Public School and Jiaotong University, renowned for mathematics and science.
4. **Xu Xu (1908–1980)**: Modern Chinese writer; his novel "Wind and Rain" (Feng Xiao Xiao), published in 1943, was one of the best-selling novels of the war years, famed for its "light melancholy, faint sorrow."
5. **The 1948 Gold Yuan Reform**: In August 1948 the Nationalist government replaced the fabi currency with gold yuan notes and forced the conversion of private gold and foreign currency. With no reserves behind it and massive overprinting, the currency collapsed within months, wiping out the savings of the urban middle class — a turning point in the era's economic collapse.
6. **The Huaihai Campaign**: November 6, 1948 – January 10, 1949, one of the decisive battles of the Chinese Civil War, fought across the Xuzhou–Bengbu region.
7. **TSMC Core Values (ICIC)**: Integrity, Commitment, Innovation, Customer Trust — the values Morris Chang established for TSMC, per public reporting.
