# Morris Chang's Parallel World · Episode 04: "Forty Job Applications"

## Opening Hook

[SFX: the clatter of a typewriter, the soft thud of envelopes, fading to a hush]

Cambridge, Massachusetts. Spring, 1955.

On the MIT campus beside the Charles River, a twenty-four-year-old man sits at a typewriter, hammering out yet another job application.

His name is Morris Chang. He has just earned a master's degree in mechanical engineering from MIT.

The typewriter is an old one; the keys clack as they strike. He had learned to type as a boy — during the months when occupied Hong Kong had kept him out of school, his father made him practice: learn a trade, and you will always be able to earn a living. That childhood skill had turned out to be his calling card.

He weighs every sentence: Dear Sir, I am a graduate of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Department of Mechanical Engineering...

Which letter is this? He has lost count. Thirty? Forty?

The five years at MIT had not been easy. After transferring from Harvard, he had buried himself in engineering: bachelor's, master's, and then he wanted to go on for a doctorate.

But he had failed the doctoral qualifying examination. Twice.

[Main narrator]

So he turned to industry — he looked for a job.

He sat down and began writing letters.

One, two, three... He mailed more than forty applications to companies large and small across America.

The replies, he would recall later, were almost nonexistent.

[Main narrator, slower]

Forty letters. Two or three replies.

This was America in 1955. The postwar economy was booming; jobs were not scarce. But for a Chinese immigrant engineer, finding a place in American industry was far harder than it looked. No doctorate, no connections, no homegrown background — all he had was an MIT diploma and a stubborn refusal to give up.

And in those forty letters, two replies changed his life.

One came from the Ford Motor Company. The other came from a company called Sylvania.

He had no idea that the decision he was typing out at this very moment would, forty years later, redraw the map of the world's chip industry.

## Act One · Five Years by the Charles

[Main narrator]

Let us rewind five years.

In the fall of 1950, Morris Chang transferred from Harvard to MIT as a sophomore in mechanical engineering.

At Harvard, he had absorbed Western culture like a sponge — literature, art, music: a year of "moveable feast." But his father's words rang in his ears: engineering is the future.

At MIT, the real grind began.

[Main narrator, slower]

MIT's course load was far heavier than Harvard's. Harvard averaged four courses a semester; MIT required five or six. Students typically studied fifty hours a week or more.

And money was always tight. His father could cover only the first year; after that, he was on his own. He lived on scholarships, typed papers for other students, and did calculation work for professors — ninety cents an hour, hour after hour at a mechanical calculator, filling page after page of dense numerical tables.

But he thrived on the hardship.

[Main narrator, slower]

MIT was a different world from Harvard.

The students were not as dazzling; they were quieter, more focused — eating, walking, talking, three sentences out of five about engineering. But the professors were true masters: Den Hartog in applied mechanics, Shapiro in fluid mechanics, Keenan in thermodynamics — men who could make the hardest material perfectly clear. No question was too trivial; if one stumped them, they would say, "Let me think it over and tell you next class" — and they never failed to return with an answer.

Chang was in his element. He had always been strong in mathematics and science, and though the work was heavy, he found it increasingly rewarding.

[Main narrator]

In 1952, he earned his bachelor's degree in mechanical engineering. In 1953, his master's.

A master's degree was already a good ticket. In the 1950s, an MIT engineering degree opened doors across American industry. But he wanted more — he wanted the doctorate.

In those days, MIT students followed a familiar path: bachelor's, master's, doctorate, in one straight run. Chang joined the line. He sat for the doctoral qualifying examination, ready to follow the road to the end.

He had always been a good student — top of his class at Harvard, strong at MIT. Failure was not something he had much experience with. The refugee boy who had crossed front lines and survived three wars had learned to expect that the world would be hard. But he had also learned, again and again, that if he worked hard enough, he could win.

And then he met the first door of his life that would not open.

## Act Two · Twice Rejected

[Main narrator]

The qualifying examination is the first gate on the road to a doctorate.

It does not test what you have learned. It tests whether you have the potential to do research — it is brutally hard, and the failure rate is high.

Chang sat for it.

He failed.

[Main narrator, slower]

He would later explain the reason with characteristic honesty: he had not prepared properly.

He had assumed that his intelligence and his MIT training would carry him through. But the qualifying exam tests genuine research potential; there is no room for luck.

He was not willing to give up. He tried again.

He failed again.

[Main narrator]

Twice rejected, the door to the doctorate closed.

Looking back years later, this failure became almost a metaphor for his whole life: his most important turning points nearly always came when his plans were broken.

In 1955, at twenty-four, he had no doctorate, no adviser, no way forward.

His parents, by then, were living in America too — they had come over in 1950. They did not pressure him. They had seen too much of the world to believe that a failed exam was a tragedy. His father, the practical banker, probably said nothing more than: find a way to make a living.

He had one choice: find a job.

And so — the forty letters.

## Act Three · The One-Dollar Fork in the Road

[Main narrator, slower]

Of the forty letters, two replies were actual offers.

The first came from Ford Motor Company.

Ford in 1955 was the symbol of American industry — the giant of Detroit. Cars rolled off its assembly lines one after another; millions of American families dreamed of owning a Ford. For a mechanical engineering master's, Ford was almost the standard answer — perfectly matched specialty, enormous platform, respectable and secure.

Chang went to Detroit for the interview and toured the vast factory. Ford's cars — he had seen them in magazines and movies since childhood in Shanghai. Now he had a chance to help build them.

Detroit in the 1950s was the motor city of the world. The assembly lines moved in precise, hypnotic rhythm; the air smelled of steel, rubber, and gasoline. It was the very heart of the American dream, and it was offering him a place inside.

Ford's offer: $479 a month.

In 1955 America, that was a respectable salary — enough for a young man to live a comfortable middle-class life.

[Main narrator, slower]

The second offer came from Sylvania Electric Products.

Sylvania was an old American maker of vacuum tubes — the glass tubes inside radios and televisions. It had just started a tiny semiconductor division, so small it was almost comical.

Sylvania's offer: $480 a month.

One dollar more than Ford.

[Main narrator]

One dollar.

Chang, of course, was not making a decision for one dollar. He valued Ford's platform far more — it was the "right path" he had dreamed of.

So he called Ford: would they raise the salary to 480, to match Sylvania?

Ford said no.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

Years later, people love to tell this story as a legend of "the one dollar that changed a life."

But what changed his life was never the dollar.

It was that he followed that dollar through a door he knew nothing about.

Behind that door was a company called Sylvania. And the division was called semiconductors.

In 1955, "semiconductor" was a word almost nobody knew how to pronounce.

In the years that followed, people asked Chang again and again: why did you choose Sylvania instead of Ford?

His answer was always honest: I had no idea what it meant. I just needed a job.

— Fate's specialty is making your most important decisions while you are not looking.

One more thing about that call to Ford. Chang made it himself, young and earnest, believing that a reasonable request would get a reasonable answer. Ford's flat refusal was a small lesson in the way the world worked: companies do not negotiate with twenty-four-year-olds who have nothing to bargain with. He never forgot it. Decades later, when he ran TSMC, he would remember what it felt like to be the one with no leverage — and he would build a company that treated its customers, however small, with a respect he had once been denied.

## Act Four · On the Eve of the Transistor Revolution

[SFX: the hum of an old radio, a glass tube glowing faintly in the distance]

[Main narrator]

To understand what kind of world Chang had walked into, we must go back eight years.

December 1947. Bell Laboratories, New Jersey.

Three physicists — Bardeen, Brattain, and Shockley — built the first transistor in human history.

It was a device the size of a fingernail, made from a sliver of germanium, that could amplify an electrical signal. It was meant to replace the clumsy, hot, unreliable vacuum tubes of the day. Those tubes had powered radios and televisions and early computers — but they burned out, wasted energy, and filled whole rooms with heat. A device that could do the same job in a sliver of material was more than an invention; it was a revolution waiting to happen.

[Main narrator, slower]

In its first few years, the transistor was a laboratory curiosity.

Then, in 1952, Bell Labs did something that shocked the industry: it opened its transistor patents to companies across America.

Forty companies each paid a $25,000 license fee and sent representatives to a nine-day transistor technology symposium. Sylvania was on that list of forty.

From that moment, the transistor moved from the laboratory to the factory floor. And America's old vacuum-tube giants — RCA, General Electric, Sylvania — rushed to transform themselves, terrified of betting on the wrong side of the technological shift.

These were companies that had ruled the vacuum-tube age. They knew one truth too well: when technology changes generations, betting wrong means death.

Sylvania's semiconductor division was born in that climate. It had no separate headquarters, no star scientists — just a group of engineers transferred from the tube workshops, and a small laboratory.

— But it was a place like this that reached out to a master's in mechanical engineering.

[Main narrator]

In 1955, Chang joined Sylvania's semiconductor division.

He would recall later that he had no idea what semiconductors were, what transistors were, or where this decision would lead.

He only thought: Sylvania sounded like a proper company, and $480 a month was enough to get by in America.

[Main narrator, slower]

On his first day, he probably walked into that small office looking utterly lost.

His assignment: improving the yield of germanium transistors.

Yield — the share of transistors that come out usable. In those days, yields were appallingly low; more than half the products were rejects. And behind that number were concrete, mechanical-engineering problems: how do you solder a wire onto a transistor without the heat destroying the device?

On the production line, a technician held a soldering iron and attached a fine wire to each transistor. The heat traveled down the wire and often burned out the delicate device.

An MIT mechanical engineering master's, assigned to a soldering problem.

Overqualified, isn't it?

But Chang did not pick and choose his work. He crouched over the production line, stared at the ruined transistors, and devised a solution: attach the wire using indirect heat, instead of direct soldering — the heat no longer destroyed the transistor.

The yield went up — not dramatically, not overnight, but steadily, because the underlying problem had been solved. It was a small victory, the kind nobody writes into history books. But it taught the young engineer a lesson he would carry for a lifetime: manufacturing is not glamorous, but it is where the value is made.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

This is worth pausing over.

Chang would spend decades in the semiconductor industry, and he repeated one idea over and over: technology has no rank; problems have no size.

A seemingly trivial soldering problem hid a delicate balance of temperature, materials, and process — the same kind of thinking he would later apply to solving yield problems at Texas Instruments, and later still to perfecting manufacturing at TSMC:

Never let a single detail pass.

His first "manufacturing miracle" was not born in a big company or a grand project. It was born on a small transistor production line, starting from a soldering problem.

## Act Five · The Teacher at the Bar

[Main narrator]

The soldering problem was solved. But Chang knew it was only a patch.

The real semiconductor theory — he knew nothing. He did not know what electrons and holes were, or why a sliver of germanium could amplify a signal.

So he began to teach himself.

He found a book — "Electrons and Holes in Semiconductors," written by William Shockley, one of the three inventors of the transistor. It was the bible of the semiconductor field.

[Main narrator, slower]

The book was too hard.

He admitted later that half of it baffled him. The concepts of energy bands, carriers, holes — they stood before him like a wall.

He could have stopped. He was just a mechanical engineer improving soldering; the yield was already up; nobody required him to understand theory.

But "good enough" was not in Chang's vocabulary.

Then a lucky accident appeared.

He discovered that a senior engineer at Sylvania spent every evening drinking at the bar of the hotel where Chang was staying, for hours at a time.

And so Chang's evenings took on a fixed rhythm —

By day, work. By night, back at the hotel, shut in his room, poring over Shockley's book for hours, writing down every problem he could not solve. Then he took his questions downstairs, to the bar, to the engineer.

[Main narrator, dialogue]

"He didn't solve all my problems, but he solved enough so I could move ahead. He was my main teacher," Chang would recall, many years later.

[Main narrator]

A young Chinese man, a small hotel room, an unreadable book, and an engineer drinking at a bar.

That was the night school of an engineer in the transistor age.

No classroom, no tuition, no diploma. Just a young man willing, on evenings when others were drinking, to trade his questions for answers.

## Epilogue · A Parallel View

[Main narrator, quiet, slower]

In 1958, after three years at Sylvania, Chang decided to leave.

He saw it clearly: this company "wasn't going anywhere" in semiconductors. Its engineers were mostly men in their fifties who had come over from vacuum tubes, and the company had little talent for marketing — it could build good transistors but could not sell them.

He accepted an invitation from Texas Instruments and moved to Dallas.

He was twenty-seven years old.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

What he did not know was that the year he left was one of the most turbulent in semiconductor history.

In October 1957, the Soviet Union had launched Sputnik — the first artificial satellite in human history. America was shaken: for the first time, Americans realized they might be falling behind in the race of science and technology.

The "Sputnik moment" changed an era. America poured money into science, engineering, and space — and the people who knew semiconductors suddenly became the most wanted talent in the country.

Chang himself probably did not realize: the little bit of semiconductor knowledge he had taught himself in that small Sylvania laboratory, in that hotel room — it was about to make him one of the most sought-after men of the new age.

[Main narrator, quiet]

Let us look back at that spring of 1955.

An MIT master's, twice rejected by the doctoral exam, had mailed forty letters and heard back from almost no one. Ford's offer had fallen through over one dollar. He had stumbled, by accident, into the semiconductor division of a company he could barely pronounce.

He thought it was the choice of a man who could not find anything better.

He did not know that behind that door was an industry about to change the world — and that he was standing, at exactly the right moment, on its threshold.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

Rejection is never the end.

The dollar Ford refused, the day Sylvania took him in, the evenings with the engineer at the bar — fate used these tiny, seemingly meaningless fragments to quietly assemble a road for him.

No one knew, at the time, where that road led.

It led to semiconductors. To Texas Instruments. To TSMC. To an old man who would one day be called the "father of the semiconductor industry."

**Rejection is not the end. It is fate pointing you down another road — and no one knew, at the time, that the road led to semiconductors.**

## Knowledge Card · Historical Background of This Episode

(Verified public facts, for the listener's reference)

1. **The Invention of the Transistor (1947)**: In December 1947, John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley of Bell Labs built the first point-contact transistor; the three shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics.
2. **The 1952 Transistor Licensing**: In April 1952, Bell Labs opened its transistor patents to 40 companies at $25,000 each and held a nine-day technology symposium — Sylvania was among the licensees. This moved the transistor from lab to factory.
3. **Sylvania's Semiconductor Division**: The old vacuum-tube maker Sylvania Electric Products started a small semiconductor division in the early 1950s (based around Ipswich/Woburn, Massachusetts). Morris Chang joined in 1955 to work on germanium transistor yield improvement.
4. **The One-Dollar Fork**: In 1955 Ford offered $479/month; Sylvania offered $480. Chang asked Ford to match, Ford refused, and he joined Sylvania (per IEEE Spectrum interview with Chang).
5. **The Sputnik Moment (1957)**: On October 4, 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite. America's shock accelerated its investment in space and technology — a defining moment of the Cold War's technology race.
6. **The Eisenhower Era (1953–1961)**: Postwar prosperity, a booming middle class, and Cold War rivalry drove rapid growth in American science, industry, and technology.
