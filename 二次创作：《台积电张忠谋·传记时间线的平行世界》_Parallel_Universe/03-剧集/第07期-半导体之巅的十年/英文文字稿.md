# Morris Chang's Parallel World · Episode 07: "The Decade at the Summit of Semiconductors"

> AIGC Parallel Biography — "The Parallel World of the TSMC Founder's Timeline"

## Opening Hook

[SFX: the low hum of electronic equipment, a distant typewriter clacking, fading down]

Dallas, Texas. 1972.

In the corridors of Texas Instruments' headquarters, a forty-one-year-old man is called into the office of the highest level.

When the door closes, he hears a new appointment: from this day on, he will run the company's entire semiconductor business — general manager of the semiconductor group.

It is the highest place he has reached in his life, so far.

[Main narrator]

The company is, at that moment, the undisputed giant of the global semiconductor industry. And this position means that in his hands is the largest chip business in the world.

No one would doubt that this is the summit of his life.

And yet the cruelest part of the story begins exactly here.

[Main narrator, slower]

Over the next dozen years, he will win nearly every battle in the marketplace. With a kind of almost ruthless pricing, he will build Texas Instruments' integrated-circuit business into the largest and most profitable in the world.

And at the same time, he will lose a battle he never even took seriously.

That battle was not fought in the marketplace. It was fought in the boardroom.

[Main narrator]

Years later, when people talk about those years, they always come back to the same question: how could a man who was invincible in the market end up losing his place inside a company?

The man's name was Morris Chang.

And this episode is the story of his decade at the summit of semiconductors — and of how, step by step, he slid from the peak toward the edge.

---

## Act One · The Learning Curve

[Main narrator]

Let us rewind a little.

In 1964, Chang came back from Stanford with his doctorate in electrical engineering and returned to Texas Instruments.

The first position the company gave him was general manager of the germanium transistor department. Under him, more than two thousand people.

[Main narrator, slower]

Note one detail that almost nobody paid attention to at the time: by the time he came back, germanium was already giving way to silicon. And yet the department he took over was still the largest and most profitable business in Texas Instruments.

He did not pick and choose. He took the department, and he ran it soundly.

Over the next few years, his post changed one after another: from germanium, to silicon, to integrated circuits. By 1967, he was sitting in the chair of general manager of the integrated-circuit department.

[Main narrator]

And it was there, in the integrated-circuit department, that he met the thing that would change his life.

That thing was called the "learning curve."

[Main narrator, slower]

At the time, the consultants from the Boston Consulting Group explained a rule to him: every time the production volume of a product doubles, its unit cost falls by a fixed percentage.

The more you make, the cheaper it gets. In the semiconductor business, that was an iron law.

Chang grasped the weight of this rule almost instantly.

He saw the whole picture in one flash: if your market share is big enough and your volume is high enough, your cost will be lower than everyone else's; with lower cost, you can cut prices harder than everyone else; and by cutting prices harder, your share grows even bigger.

It was a snowballing loop. Whoever got it turning first would win.

[Main narrator]

So he did something the whole industry thought was madness.

He decided: every quarter, cut prices on his own initiative — even when the market was not asking for it, even when this quarter's profit would be thinner because of it.

He would use price to drive his rivals off the table, one by one.

[Main narrator, slower]

At the time, this looked almost like a suicidal strategy. The board did not understand it, and competitors understood it even less.

Only Chang himself knew: what he wanted was not this quarter's profit, but the card table ten years from now — and by then, he wanted to be the only one left sitting at it.

To support this strategy, he also built a new factory in Houston. Its yield reached forty percent from the very start — in an era when everyone else was struggling just to reach twenty percent, that was almost a miracle.

[Main narrator]

That strategy, "learning-curve pricing," ultimately made Texas Instruments' integrated-circuit business the largest and most profitable in the world.

And in that same battle, he slowly shaped his own management philosophy.

[Main narrator, slower]

He believed that management rests not on authority, but on leadership. He disliked hierarchies that crushed people; he preferred a flat organization. He did not care about background or seniority — only about whether a person could actually do the job.

And this, too, was something he had learned from Texas Instruments itself.

[Main narrator]

Texas Instruments in those years was a company where young people did not want to go home. Up and down the ranks, almost everyone was under forty, and everyone worked fifty hours a week or more. Executives had no reserved parking spaces. Managers and workers ate in the same cafeteria, at the same tables.

The hierarchy existed, but the wall of status was low.

Chang carved this temperament into his own way of managing.

Years later, when he founded TSMC on the other side of the strait, people would notice that the company still carried this flavor — promote the capable, keep the structure flat.

## Act Two · Facing Intel

[Main narrator]

While Chang was pouring himself into the integrated-circuit business, something was happening outside that he did not take too seriously at first.

In 1971, a small company just three years old released a thing the size of a fingernail.

The small company was called Intel. The thing was called a "microprocessor."

[Main narrator, slower]

It was the first microprocessor a person could buy. A single chip that was the central processing unit of a computer.

To many people, it looked like a curious little gadget. But Chang and Texas Instruments soon realized: the wind was turning.

Because in 1974, Intel brought out another chip called the 8080. This one was powerful enough to drive a real computer.

The leadership of Texas Instruments could no longer sit still.

[Main narrator]

In those years, an order came down inside Texas Instruments: catch up to Intel on microprocessors.

So the engineers in Dallas worked overtime, determined to beat Intel to something more advanced — a sixteen-bit microprocessor, code-named TMS9900.

Texas Instruments was the largest semiconductor company in the world; Intel was only a startup. In resources, in scale, Texas Instruments was several times Intel's size.

And yet the result of the race surprised everyone.

[Main narrator, slower]

In 1976, the TMS9900 was finally ready. But it carried flaws it was born with: no matching sixteen-bit peripheral chips, and an address space of only sixteen bits — so the memory it could manage was painfully small.

Two years later, in April 1978, Intel brought out its own sixteen-bit chip, the 8086. Its address space was twenty bits, and the memory it could reach was sixteen times what the Texas Instruments chip could manage.

One step behind, and the gap had become a chasm.

[Main narrator]

Three years later, when IBM set out to build its first personal computer, it chose Intel's chip over Texas Instruments'.

That choice settled the world for decades to come.

The age of the personal computer had arrived. And the "heart" of that age belonged to Intel, not to Texas Instruments.

[Main narrator, slower]

And, by the way, this long race was wrapped inside an even larger era.

In 1973, the oil crisis struck. Oil prices soared, and the American economy fell into a strange new condition called "stagflation" — prices rising while the economy stood still.

The semiconductor business, almost overnight, went from a blue ocean to a hard war in which everyone had to tighten their belts.

In the market, the wolves were multiplying. And what Chang had to face was not only the wolves outside.

---

## Act Three · The Defeat in the Boardroom

[Main narrator]

In 1972, Chang was promoted to general manager of the semiconductor group.

It should have been his proudest moment. But it was precisely from this moment that he began to feel something he could not control.

[Main narrator, slower]

The first bad news came from a chip called the "16K memory."

It was the most advanced product of the time, and the number-one project of the semiconductor group. And that project failed.

Failure itself was not the worst part. The worst part was what happened next — company leadership, going around him, set up a parallel project in another place, to copy a competitor's product.

Chang understood exactly what that meant: the company no longer trusted him.

[Main narrator]

And the tension between him and his immediate boss only deepened.

His boss had no background in semiconductors. The two men looked at problems in ways that were often worlds apart.

Chang proposed buying more advanced equipment from outside vendors, instead of sticking with the company's own aging, internally designed machines.

It was rejected.

Chang proposed increasing R&D spending, arguing that short-term profit must not be traded away for long-term competitiveness.

Rejected again.

[Main narrator, slower]

What chilled him most was an order.

Company leadership demanded that he allocate semiconductor capacity to the company's own products first.

Chang refused. He insisted that capacity should be divided fairly between internal customers and external customers.

That order became the last straw. From then on, company leadership began to criticize him openly.

[Main narrator]

To be fair, Texas Instruments in those years was indeed in decline.

It sat in Texas, far from Silicon Valley, and found it harder and harder to attract the best talent. And across the Pacific, Japan, under strong government support, was quietly assembling a huge semiconductor army.

In 1976, Japan launched the "VLSI" program that would later send a chill through all of America — five Japanese giants joining forces, with government money, charging together at the highest summit of semiconductor technology.

Intel in front, Japan behind. And Chang was trapped in an ever-smaller boardroom, fighting a war he could never win against his own boss.

[Main narrator, slower]

In 1977, he made a decision.

He asked, on his own initiative, to leave the semiconductor group.

---

## Act Four · Exile

[Main narrator]

The company moved him to the consumer products group.

On paper, it was another big group, and the title was not low. But Chang understood exactly what kind of place it was.

[Main narrator, slower]

Consumer products was Texas Instruments' ambitious attempt to stop being merely a supplier to other companies, and instead to sell products directly to ordinary people.

And the whole game was going badly.

When Chang took over, the group had three product lines: simple consumer calculators, more advanced scientific calculators, and electronic watches.

Of the three, only the scientific calculators made money. And the money they made was tiny. The whole group was losing.

[Main narrator]

He did not agree with the company's strategy of pushing into consumer goods. In his view, it was a road that led nowhere.

But he had taken the job, so he could only grit his teeth and sort through the mess, one piece at a time.

In those years, the only thing the group could really be proud of was a toy called the Speak & Spell — a little machine that could talk, and that taught children how to spell.

The speech-synthesis chip inside it was so hard to make that competitors could not copy it for a while. And so it became the single point of light in those lost years.

[Main narrator, slower]

But one point of light could not save the whole situation.

A man who had spent his life making chips, who treated yield as an article of faith, was dropped into a swamp where he had to guess what consumers wanted. The more expert he was, the less his expertise mattered.

He could still read the market. But where the company wanted him to go, and what it wanted him to become — that he could read less and less.

[Main narrator]

Years later, when people look back at Chang's final years at Texas Instruments, they use one word: exile.

It was not yet the moment he finally left. The real departure would come a few years later — in 1983, when he would walk out of the company's gate carrying the regrets of twenty-five years.

But the foreshadowing of that fate was already laid down here.

[Main narrator, slower]

Because it was in these exiled years that Chang began to turn one question over and over in his mind:

How could a man who had won every battle in the market lose so completely inside a company?

The answer to that question would, many years later, redraw the map of the entire world's semiconductor industry.

## Epilogue · A Parallel View

[Main narrator, quiet, slower]

Let us look at these years once more, from the beginning.

In 1964, he came back with a Stanford doctorate, started from a germanium transistor department of two thousand people, and climbed all the way to general manager of the semiconductor group of the world's largest semiconductor company.

He invented a strategy called learning-curve pricing and made Texas Instruments' integrated-circuit business number one in the world.

In the marketplace, he almost never lost.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

And yet history chose to let him lose elsewhere.

He lost to Intel — not in technology, but in a design flaw that chip was born with.

And he lost to the company where he had spent a quarter of a century — not in ability, but in a power game inside one boardroom.

[Main narrator, quiet]

We like to say that business is a battlefield.

But Chang, in this decade of his life, taught us something else: **the hardest battles are not fought in the marketplace — they are fought in the boardroom.** The market has rules, has rivals, has outcomes you can calculate; but the game in the boardroom has no rules, no referee, only human hearts.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

And yet it was this very defeat that made the man he would become.

If he had sailed smoothly up Texas Instruments to its very top, there would be no TSMC.

It was precisely because he was exiled, pushed to the edge, that in his fifties he reset himself to zero, crossed an ocean, and returned to that island called Taiwan to start over from nothing.

[Main narrator, quiet]

The man who lost in the boardroom came to understand three things:

First, technology changes and markets change, but the judgment of talent can never change.

Second, a company's governance structure decides its life and death even before its technology does.

And third, the strongest card in your hand is not the power you hold, but the courage to start over at any time.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

All three of these he later carried with him to TSMC.

So when we look back today at that contest at the summit of semiconductors in the 1970s, we see a picture full of meaning:

Intel won the microprocessor. The Japanese army won the memory chip. And Morris Chang lost a game inside his own company.

But the man who lost that game was the one who finally won the chance to rewrite an entire industry.

**He won nearly every battle in the marketplace, yet he lost the power game inside Texas Instruments. The hardest battles are not fought in the marketplace — they are fought in the boardroom. And that lesson taught him, later, how to build a mountain.**

## Knowledge Card · Historical Background of This Episode

(Verified public facts, for the listener's reference)

1. **Chang's Rise at TI**: He joined Texas Instruments in 1958; after returning in 1964 he became general manager of the germanium transistor department, general manager of the integrated-circuit department in 1967, and group vice president and general manager of the semiconductor group in 1972; his TI career spanned 25 years (1958–1983).
2. **Learning-Curve Pricing**: Working with the Boston Consulting Group, Chang pushed "proactive quarterly price cuts" in TI's integrated-circuit business, trading short-term profit for market share and long-term cost advantage, and made TI's IC business the world's largest and most profitable at the time.
3. **The Microprocessor Age**: In 1971 Intel released the 4004, the first commercial microprocessor; in 1974 it released the 8080; TI's 16-bit TMS9900 was ready in 1976; in April 1978 Intel released the 8086 (with a 20-bit address space); in 1981 IBM chose Intel's 8088 for its first PC.
4. **The 1973 Oil Crisis**: The OPEC oil embargo sent prices soaring, and the United States entered a 1970s era of "stagflation" (rising prices combined with economic stagnation), raising cost pressure on manufacturing and consumer electronics.
5. **The Rise of Japanese Semiconductors**: In 1971 MITI reorganized Japan's computer industry; in March 1976 it launched the VLSI program, joining Fujitsu, Hitachi, Matsushita, Mitsubishi, and NEC, and with government backing Japan's global semiconductor share surged.
6. **TI's Consumer Group and the Speak & Spell**: Chang asked for a transfer in 1977 and became head of the consumer products group, whose three product lines had only the scientific calculators turning a profit; the one bright spot of his tenure was the 1978 talking spelling toy, the Speak & Spell.
7. **TI's Flat Culture**: Texas Instruments in its early years was staffed mostly by people under forty working 50-plus hours a week, with no reserved parking for executives and executives eating alongside workers; Chang's management philosophy centered on "leadership rather than authority," flat organization, and meritocracy.
