# Morris Chang's Parallel World · Episode 10: "From Taiwan to the World"

> AIGC Parallel Biography — "The Parallel World of the TSMC Founder's Timeline"
> This episode's lens: **When the storm comes, it is the solid company that gets seen.**
> Target length: 20 minutes (English ~3,100 words)
> Original creative work based on public record; no text from any copyrighted book is reproduced.

## Opening Hook

[SFX: the opening bell of a stock exchange, the murmur of a crowd, fading down]

1997. A storm is sweeping in from Southeast Asia.

Thailand's currency collapses. Indonesia's collapses. South Korea's collapses. Stock markets crash, factories shut their doors, and in a single night, the whole of Asia falls into panic.

[Main narrator, slower]

On the stock screens, a sea of red. Outside factory gates, long lines of workers waiting for wages. In the airports, investors clutching luggage, desperate to move their money out.

In that year, the prosperity of East Asia shattered like a mirror.

[Main narrator]

And yet, in the middle of all this ruin, one company does something that surprises everyone.

It does not shrink. It does not retreat. Instead, it steps onto the largest capital stage in the world.

[Main narrator, slower]

In October 1997, this company lists on the New York Stock Exchange — becoming the first Taiwanese company ever to be listed in the United States.

The company is TSMC.

[Main narrator]

Ten years earlier, it had been a small company starting from an old production line, laughed at by the whole industry. Ten years later, it was the largest foundry in the world.

And its founder was a man who only started his company at the age of fifty-six.

[Main narrator, slower]

And this is the final episode of our first volume.

It is the story of a man who walked out of the fires of war, and of the company he built — and of how they stood on the world's stage in the middle of the Asian financial storm.

## Act One · The Storm Arrives

[Main narrator]

On July 2, 1997, Thailand abandoned the fixed exchange rate it had held for decades.

The baht plunged.

[Main narrator, slower]

On its own, it sounded like a small financial event. But the chain reaction it set off fell across East Asia like a row of dominoes.

Indonesia, Malaysia, South Korea — one currency after another collapsed like an avalanche. Stock markets were cut in half, banks failed, and countless companies became insolvent overnight.

This was what the world would come to call the Asian financial crisis.

[Main narrator, slower]

And here is the cruel part: just a few years before the storm, people were still calling this region by another name — the "Asian miracle."

For decades, from Japan and Korea to Southeast Asia, these countries and regions had ridden exports and an endless tide of hot money, charging forward, held up as models for the world to follow.

But the tide that rushes in also rushes out. The moment investors began to doubt whether these currencies could hold, a stampede began. Hot money fled for the exits, currencies plunged, and the prosperity that had been built on borrowed money turned, overnight, into ruins weighed down by debt.

The storm finally dragged Asia into its deepest valley in decades.

[Main narrator, slower]

The International Monetary Fund rushed in and reached out to several countries. But its help came with conditions — austerity, reform, restructuring — at a heavy cost.

It was a lesson the whole of Asia would remember for many years.

[Main narrator]

Taiwan, too, felt the chill.

[Main narrator, slower]

Taiwan's stock market fell, and the Taiwan dollar slid. But compared with its neighbors, who had fallen into the abyss, Taiwan held its ground.

Because it had built up hundreds of billions of dollars in foreign reserves — and it had raised a whole generation of enterprises that actually created value.

And TSMC was the hardest, most solid piece of ballast among them.

[Main narrator]

Yet, to many people's surprise, TSMC's days in the storm were not too hard.

The reason was simple: most of TSMC's customers were fabless design houses in Silicon Valley. They were spread across the world, and their orders were not tied to any single region. And chips, in that era, were among the last things anyone had trouble selling.

The storm came — but the orders in TSMC's hands were still there.

[Main narrator, slower]

And this was not really luck.

From its very first day, TSMC had walked a "global-facing" road. It never staked its life on any single market; instead, it scattered its customers across every corner of the world.

A storm can overturn one region — but it cannot overturn a net cast across the whole world.

## Act Two · The Bell in New York

[Main narrator]

In October 1997, TSMC did something big.

It issued American Depositary Receipts and listed on the New York Stock Exchange. Its ticker: TSM. The offering price: twenty-four dollars and seventy-eight cents per ADR.

[Main narrator, slower]

It was the first Taiwanese company ever listed in the United States.

Let us pause to explain what an "American Depositary Receipt" is.

Simply put, it is a foreign company's shares, repackaged into certificates that trade on American exchanges. It works like a bridge, connecting a factory in Taiwan with the capital of Wall Street.

Before TSMC, the companies that walked onto that bridge were mostly giants from Japan and Europe. TSMC was the first Taiwanese company ever to cross it.

[Main narrator, slower]

In fact, three years earlier, TSMC had already listed on the Taiwan Stock Exchange. That was its first step into its home capital market.

Now, it was knocking on the door of the whole world.

[Main narrator]

Consider the weight of that "first."

In the capital world of America, a chip company from Taiwan could now be bought and sold by investors all over the world, just like Intel, just like IBM.

[Main narrator, slower]

For TSMC, this was more than a new source of funding.

It meant that the company the whole industry had once laughed at had formally stepped onto the world's stage. Its name now stood side by side with the giants of Silicon Valley.

[Main narrator]

And for Morris Chang, it was a moment full of meaning.

Long ago, he had been a child fleeing through the fires of war. Later, he was a young man crossing an ocean to study in the West. Still later, he was the passed-over executive, exiled at Texas Instruments.

And now, at sixty-six, he stood at the center of the world.

[Main narrator, slower]

Someone asked him: why list the company in America at this very moment?

His answer was plain to the point of bluntness: a company that wants to truly grow cannot stay in its own pond. It must swim in the deepest sea in the world.

For it is in the deepest sea that the greatest storms rise — and where the biggest fish are found.

## Act Three · The Foundational Year

[Main narrator]

1998 was a special year in Chang's life.

That year, BusinessWeek named him one of the "Top 25 Managers of the Year."

[Main narrator, slower]

The weight of that honor lay in where it came from — from across the ocean, from the Western world he had once tried so hard to join, and from which he had once returned disappointed.

Now the Western world reached out, and handed that recognition back to him.

And of the other twenty-four names on that list, almost all were American business giants. Morris Chang was one of the very few Eastern faces on it.

[Main narrator]

The honors kept coming.

The next year, 1999, the Fabless Semiconductor Association gave its first-ever Exemplary Leadership Award to Chang — an award that would later be renamed after him.

And in 2000, the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers gave him the Robert N. Noyce Medal, one of the highest honors in the semiconductor world.

[Main narrator, slower]

And in that same year, 1998, Chang did something that seemed almost out of place in the business world.

He published his autobiography — volume one.

[Main narrator]

That first volume covers the years 1931 to 1964 — from his birth in the chaos of war, to flight, to studying in America, to joining Texas Instruments, and finally to earning his doctorate at Stanford.

It was the story of his first half of life.

[Main narrator, slower]

In that book, he wrote down the shape of the road he had walked.

He wrote of fleeing through the flames of war, of Nankai Middle School in Chongqing, of a boy who had dreamed of being a writer before his father talked him back into engineering.

He wrote of the year at Harvard, of the decision to transfer to MIT, of failing the doctoral exam twice and still forcing his way into the semiconductor industry with dozens of job applications.

He also wrote of twenty-five years at Texas Instruments — rising from fixing yields, to learning-curve pricing, to facing down Intel, to that final defeat in the boardroom.

And all of it stops in 1964 — the moment he received his Stanford doctorate.

Volume one ended there. Just as his life had only reached its halfway point.

[Main narrator]

Many people wondered: how does the man running the world's largest foundry find the time to write a book about the past?

But Chang understood his purpose. He was not writing only about himself.

[Main narrator, slower]

And there was a more personal reason, too.

In his life, Chang had met too many people, and said goodbye to too many. Family scattered by war. Classmates tossed about by a great era. Colleagues who had walked beside him in the semiconductor industry and then drifted away.

He feared that if he did not write them down, their names would slowly be erased by time.

So that autobiography is the story of one life — and of the fates of a whole generation.

## Act Four · Steadiness in the Storm

[Main narrator]

Let us turn the camera back to the storm.

In the two fiercest years of the Asian financial crisis, countless companies fell. But TSMC did not fall. Instead, it quietly grew stronger in the middle of the storm.

[Main narrator, slower]

Its secret was not mysterious at all.

While others shrank in panic, TSMC held fast to the things it cared about most: yield, cost, and that promise never to compete with its customers.

[Main narrator, slower]

Those three things were a moat that TSMC had spent ten years building, brick by brick. When the storm came, other walls were shaking — but its moat did not move.

[Main narrator]

And while Asia was sinking into the storm, something else was quietly beginning across the ocean.

The internet was seeing its first dawn. In the garages of Silicon Valley, one new company after another was springing up — to build websites, to build networking gear, to build everything that had anything to do with getting connected.

And every single one of those things needed chips.

[Main narrator, slower]

The storm in the East and the dawn in the West overlapped in a strange and wonderful way. The storm could not bring TSMC down, while the dawn kept urging it to run faster.

[Main narrator]

The storm stripped bare, one by one, the companies that had been propped up by luck and bubbles.

And the truly solid companies — after the storm passed — were seen clearly by the whole world.

[Main narrator, slower]

That is the truth behind the old saying: only when the tide goes out do you see who has been swimming naked.

And when the storm comes, it is the solid company that gets seen.

[Main narrator]

After the storm, the world did its sums again: who was swimming naked, and who was wearing trunks.

The companies built on hot money and bubbles lay in ruins. But TSMC, with its solid yields, its healthy customer base, and a promise it never broke, was re-priced by the world's capital.

To be seen is not to be flattered. It is to be weighed again by the most demanding eyes — and to still win their nod.

In the storm that swept across Asia in 1997, the world looked at TSMC again — and saw it for what it truly was.

## Epilogue · A Parallel View

[Main narrator, quiet, slower]

Here, the story of our first volume draws to a close.

Let us walk the whole timeline once more, from the beginning.

In 1931, a boy was born into an age of war. He fled. He studied. He crossed an ocean. Inside a Western company, he rose from fixing yields all the way to the peak of the semiconductor business.

Then he was exiled. He left. And at fifty-six, he returned to the East and founded a company that nobody believed in.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

That company designed no chips. It only made other people's chips.

And yet it was this very idea of "the foundry" that let chip designers, for the first time, live without a factory — and that let an island known only for plastic toys and cheap running shoes climb, for the first time, to the summit of world technology.

[Main narrator, quiet]

Morris Chang's biography is a mirror.

In that mirror, we see more than one man's fate. We see an era: a child of the East walking through the fires of war into the classrooms of the West — and then carrying everything the West gave him back to the East, to bind the two worlds together again.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

What kind of spirit is this?

It is the spirit of endless striving, of forever hunting for the next opportunity, of pushing the boundaries of one's ability ever outward.

And it is the courage of a man rebuilding himself, in a foreign land, from the ground up.

[Main narrator, quiet]

That is why we named this first volume "From Taiwan to the World."

But the title can also be read in reverse: from the world, back to Taiwan.

One man crossed between civilizations twice in his life: in his youth, from East to West; past fifty, from West back to East. And in neither crossing did he return empty-handed.

He carried away a full set of skills — and he carried back an era.

[Main narrator, quiet]

And what he carried back was TSMC, was Hsinchu, was the science park the world would one day watch — and behind it, the fate of an entire generation.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

If I had to sum up these ten episodes in three sentences, I would put it this way:

First, your starting point does not decide your destination — the boy who fled the war would become the "godfather" of the world's semiconductors.

Second, the hardest battles are not fought in the market, but in the human heart — he had to lose before he truly learned to win.

Third, the disruptor does not play the hero — he builds the platform, and only by making everyone else the hero does he become irreplaceable.

[Main narrator, quiet]

And all of this, in the "foundational year" of 1998, was still only the beginning.

Because the real legend of TSMC had only just begun to climb toward its climax.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

In the stories to come, you will see this company cross the millennium, survive the bursting of the internet bubble, and grow stronger through crisis after crisis.

And you will see it grow from a "foundry" into something the whole world cannot do without — the company they call the "sacred mountain that shields the nation."

**When the storm comes, it is the solid company that gets seen — and after the storm, the solid man and his solid company would raise a mountain for the whole world to look up to.**

[Main narrator, quiet]

This is the ten episodes of our first volume.

From the birth of a child in 1931, to the laying of a mountain's foundation in 1998.

We have told the story of one man — and of a whole era's parallel world.

Thank you for listening this far. We will meet again in Volume Two.

## Knowledge Card · Historical Background of This Episode

(Verified public facts, for the listener's reference)

1. **The Asian Financial Crisis**: On July 2, 1997, Thailand abandoned its fixed exchange rate and the baht devalued, triggering a crisis that spread across Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia, and the rest of East Asia.
2. **TSMC Lists on the NYSE**: In October 1997, TSMC issued American Depositary Receipts (ADRs) and listed on the New York Stock Exchange, becoming the first Taiwanese company listed in the United States; the offering price was $24.78 per ADR.
3. **Top 25 Managers of the Year**: In 1998, BusinessWeek named Morris Chang one of its "Top 25 Managers of the Year."
4. **The Autobiography, Volume One**: In 1998, Chang published the first volume of his autobiography, covering 1931–1964 — his birth, flight, education, years at Texas Instruments, and Stanford doctorate.
5. **Later Honors**: In 1999, Chang received the first Exemplary Leadership Award of the Fabless Semiconductor Association (an award later named after him); in 2000 he received the IEEE Robert N. Noyce Medal.
6. **TSMC Revenue**: 1996 revenue was about $1.45 billion; 1997 revenue was about $1.37 billion, dipping slightly under the impact of the Asian financial crisis.
7. **Industry Position**: By the late 1990s, TSMC was the world's largest dedicated foundry, serving mostly fabless design houses around the globe.
