# Morris Chang's Parallel World · Episode 08: "Leaving Texas & the Invitation Home"

> AIGC Parallel Biography — "The Parallel World of the TSMC Founder's Timeline"

## Opening Hook

[SFX: the faint echo of airport announcements, the rolling of luggage wheels, fading down]

Dallas, Texas. 1983.

A man who has worked for Texas Instruments for twenty-five years walks out of the company gate. In his hands, there is no offer for the next job.

This year he is fifty-two. Inside one company he has climbed from the very bottom to group vice president, running the largest semiconductor business in the world. And now, his hands are empty.

[Main narrator]

Nobody knows that two years later he will board a plane heading east. And nobody knows that four years later, on a small island, he will found a company that will rewrite the world.

[Main narrator, slower]

Many years later, when people look back at this life, they will see one long, meaningful arc:

A Chinese man who had spent half his life working in the West — at fifty-four, he left a senior executive post at a major American company, and staked the second half of his life on a small island called Taiwan.

What he staked was not the rest of his life. It was the future of an industry.

[Main narrator]

The man's name was Morris Chang.

And this episode is the story of how he left Texas — and how he was invited back home.

---

## Act One · The End of Exile

[Main narrator]

Let us go back to the last years at Texas Instruments.

In 1981, Chang was moved away from the semiconductor business he knew so well, and given a new title: director of quality and productivity.

The title sounded impressive. But he knew exactly what it was — a position that looked like a promotion but was really a shelf, a place where you were set aside and left alone.

[Main narrator, slower]

Before this, he had already stumbled once in the consumer group. In 1979, Texas Instruments released a home computer called the TI-99/4. Chang had opposed the project at the time — and he was proven right: it sold terribly, fewer than twenty thousand units, and became an embarrassment for the company. Yet in the end, the blame landed on him.

[Main narrator]

In later years, when he remembered this period, he used one heavy word: exile.

In those final years at Texas Instruments, his bonus was cut, and his seniority was reduced. He knew it was only a matter of time — sooner or later, the company would take away his stock options, and then politely ask him to retire.

[Main narrator]

And yet, he had spent twenty-five years at this company. Leaving was not easy for him.

But in the end, he could not swallow it any longer.

In 1983, he resigned from Texas Instruments. On the day he left, he had no offer for the next job.

[Main narrator, slower]

In his own words — he left Texas Instruments with nothing.

Fifty-two years old. At that age, most people are already thinking about retirement. But Morris Chang stood at a crossroads.

---

## Act Two · A Year in New York

[Main narrator]

After resigning, he looked at several jobs. One of them was running Kodak's new digital photography division.

But in the end, he accepted a different post: president of General Instrument.

The company made electronic products, and it was no small outfit. He moved to New York City, into the fifty-third floor of Trump Tower — his next-door neighbor was the man the building was named after.

[Main narrator, slower]

On the surface, it looked like a respectable new start. But Chang soon found that things were not what he expected.

He thought his job was to build up General Instrument's most profitable businesses. But gradually he understood that the CEO who hired him wanted something else entirely — to buy small companies, repackage them, and then turn around and sell them, like a private-equity firm.

The two men's ideas ran in opposite directions.

[Main narrator]

In less than a year, he was asked to resign.

That year at General Instrument was not a happy one. And yet, in that short year, he met a man — and heard one sentence — that would change the second half of his life.

[Main narrator, slower]

At one point, an entrepreneur came to him, trying to raise fifty million dollars to start a chip-design company.

But not long afterward, the entrepreneur came back and told Chang: I don't need the money anymore.

Why? Because he had figured something out — he did not need to build an expensive fabrication plant of his own. He only had to design the chip, and outsource the manufacturing to some other semiconductor company.

[Main narrator]

That entrepreneur was Gordon Campbell. The company he later founded, Chips and Technologies, was one of the first "fabless" semiconductor companies in the world — it designed chips, but owned no factory.

When Chang heard this, he did not immediately realize that this idea would one day redraw the map of the whole semiconductor industry.

But he remembered it.

[Main narrator, slower]

Hold on to this detail. Many years later, when Chang founded TSMC in Taiwan, the very sentence turning in his mind would be this one — the people who make chips and the people who design them can be separated.

---

## Act Three · Going Home

[Main narrator]

Now let us turn the camera from New York to the other side of the Pacific.

In the years when Chang was still struggling at Texas Instruments, Taiwan was quietly playing a much bigger game.

[Main narrator, slower]

Taiwan in those days was a place that had made its living from plastic toys and cheap running shoes.

[Main narrator]

Over those decades, Taiwan had walked a rare road: from a postwar agricultural society, it turned itself into a manufacturing workshop for the whole world. By the 1970s it had a thriving export industry, and it had raised a generation of engineers and technocrats.

But the words "contract manufacturing" were a thorn in its side — it could make things, but the technology was never in its own hands. Taiwan's decision-makers understood more and more clearly: to truly stand on its own feet, it had to master a core technology like semiconductors.

[Main narrator, slower]

It wanted to move up — to climb from low-end manufacturing to high technology. But making chips was no small thing; it was the most advanced technology on earth.

So in 1973, Taiwan founded the Industrial Technology Research Institute. And in 1976, ITRI bought an already-outdated seven-micron semiconductor process from America's RCA, and built its first production line with it.

[Main narrator]

In 1980, a place called the Hsinchu Science Park broke ground, rising out of rice paddies and sugarcane fields. It would come to be called Taiwan's "Silicon Valley."

And behind all of this stood one key figure — K.T. Li.

[Main narrator, slower]

Li Kwoh-ting, known to the world as K.T. Li, has been called the "father of Taiwan's economic miracle." He led the founding of the Hsinchu Science Park, and he drove the takeoff of Taiwan's high-tech industry.

And he had his eye on Morris Chang for a long time.

[Main narrator]

In fact, as early as 1982 — before Chang had even left Texas Instruments — Taiwan had already invited him to come back and head ITRI. But that time, he could not reach a workable arrangement with his boss, and the matter faded away.

Three years later, after he left General Instrument and once again stood at an empty moment in his life, the invitation from the east came again.

[Main narrator, slower]

This time, more than one name stood behind it: the support of Premier Yu Kuo-hwa, the leadership of ITRI chairman Hsu Hsien-hsiu, and above all, K.T. Li pushing with all his might from behind the scenes.

In 1985, at fifty-four, Morris Chang boarded a plane for Taiwan and took office as president of the Industrial Technology Research Institute.

[Main narrator]

And just as he was boarding, a storm was gathering across the Pacific.

In 1986, the United States and Japan signed a semiconductor agreement — Japan agreed to open its market and to stop dumping chips at cutthroat prices. A trade war over semiconductor supremacy was burning at full heat.

[Main narrator, slower]

In those years, a kind of "Japan anxiety" hung over the whole of America. The memory chips Japan made were cheap and good, and they beat American chipmakers back step by step — Intel was driven out of the memory business, and Texas Instruments was badly hurt.

That semiconductor war between the United States and Japan became one of the most famous lessons in the history of the industry. And one of its unintended consequences was that Taiwan quietly stepped into the wind.

[Main narrator]

America's chip giants were busy fighting the Japanese army. None of them would have guessed that, at that very moment, a fifty-four-year-old Chinese man was quietly planting another seed on a small island.

---

## Act Four · Three Requests

[Main narrator]

On his second day at ITRI, Chang received a to-do list left behind by his predecessor.

The first item on the list gave him a headache: three chip companies had all asked, at the same time, to build their own fabrication plants.

[Main narrator, slower]

None of the three would back down. But building a separate plant for each of them would cost more than a hundred million dollars — an astronomical sum for ITRI.

Refuse, and you are pushing away the very industry ITRI exists to serve. Agree, and where does the money come from?

Everyone thought it was an unsolvable puzzle.

[Main narrator]

Only Chang saw a third way.

He knew this industry inside out. He knew that the cost of building a fab was climbing year after year. He also knew that a plant must reach a large enough output before its costs could fall — the very "learning curve" he had learned at Texas Instruments.

And the fabs Taiwan had now were all far too small.

[Main narrator, slower]

So he made a proposal that stunned everyone:

Don't build three plants. Build one. A single "common fab."

This plant would belong to no single company, and it would not design chips itself. It would do exactly one thing — manufacture chips for other people. Bring your design, and I will make it for you.

[Main narrator]

That was the seed of the "foundry" model that would one day shake the world: a company that designs nothing, sells no chips of its own, competes with no customer — it only focuses on manufacturing the chips that other people have designed.

At the time, the whole industry treated the idea as fantasy. But Chang saw it clearly: fabs were getting more and more expensive, so fewer and fewer companies would want to build their own; while more and more companies would want to design chips and own no factory at all.

He was betting on the future.

[Main narrator, slower]

Why was he the only one who could think of this model?

Because he knew the pain of this industry better than anyone. He remembered that Texas Instruments had once been sued — accused of unfairly giving priority to its own products. TI won that lawsuit only because Chang had refused, years earlier, to carry out that very order to favor internal customers.

So he understood: a company that both designs chips and makes them for others will always leave its customers anxious. Only a company that designs nothing, and only makes things for others, can persuade customers to hand over their designs without fear.

He also knew another secret: Asian fabs often reached higher yields than American ones. ITRI's technology was behind, but its yields were first-class.

---

## Act Five · TSMC Is Born

[Main narrator]

He had the idea. Now, where would the money come from?

Chang knew that government money alone would not be enough — private capital had to come in. But private investors would only put up money if the project looked solid.

So he needed a serious semiconductor manufacturer to come in as a partner.

[Main narrator, slower]

He went knocking, one door at a time.

Intel — refused. Texas Instruments — refused. Motorola — refused. AMD, Panasonic, Sony — every one of them shook their heads.

And they all gave the same reason: why would we invest money to help a potential competitor manufacture chips?

[Main narrator]

In the end, only one company said yes — Philips, of the Netherlands.

What Philips saw was less the foundry idea itself, and more Taiwan's cheap labor and its government connections. But either way, it put up the money.

[Main narrator, slower]

There is a certain irony here: Philips itself was only a second-rate player in semiconductors. It said yes largely to please Taiwan's government, and to make use of the island's cheap labor.

But it was this not-quite-pure investment that gave TSMC its first breath of life.

[Main narrator, slower]

The final capital structure looked like this: the government put in about seventy million dollars, becoming the largest shareholder with roughly forty-eight percent; Philips put in about forty million, roughly twenty-seven and a half percent; and the rest came from private investors.

In 1986, Taiwan's first six-inch integrated-circuit pilot plant was completed.

On February 21, 1987, the new company was officially established.

[Main narrator]

It had a long name — Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company. Years later, the world would know it by a simpler one: TSMC.

Morris Chang, at fifty-six, became its chairman.

[Main narrator, slower]

On that day, almost nobody thought much of it.

It had no chip designs of its own, no advanced technology to speak of, and its production line was just an old line repurposed from ITRI. The whole industry was waiting to see how soon it would collapse.

But deep in Chang's mind was a judgment almost nobody believed:

In the future, thousands upon thousands of companies would design chips and own no factory. And TSMC was built for them.

---

## Epilogue · A Parallel View

[Main narrator, quiet, slower]

Let us look at this life once more, from the beginning.

In 1955, a young man out of MIT stumbled into the semiconductor business over a difference of one dollar.

For the next twenty-five years, at Texas Instruments, he rose from fixing yields all the way to running the largest semiconductor business in the world.

And in the end, he was exiled, pushed to the edge, and left at fifty-two with nothing in his hands.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

Here history left a turn full of meaning.

If he had sailed smoothly up Texas Instruments to its very top, there would be no TSMC.

It was precisely those two departures — leaving Texas, leaving General Instrument — that pushed him into a position no one else had ever stood in.

[Main narrator, quiet]

We like to say that people should always climb higher.

But Morris Chang, with the choice he made at fifty-four, taught us something else: **sometimes the most important move in life is leaving the wrong position.**

He was not stepping down. He was changing tables.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

He left the Western world he knew so well, and returned to the East where he was born. It was another journey across civilizations — except this time, the direction was reversed.

As a young man, he had walked out of the fires of war in the East, toward the classrooms and laboratories of the West. At fifty-four, he carried everything the West had taught him back to the East, determined to build a new world there.

[Main narrator, quiet]

A homecomer's gamble is never only about personal glory.

What Chang staked was the future of an industry. His bet was this: could a people who had once made only plastic toys and cheap running shoes climb to the summit of world technology — with one factory that manufactured other people's chips?

[Main narrator, with feeling]

We all know how the story turned out.

The company nobody believed in took less than ten years to reach a billion dollars in annual revenue. A few decades later, it became the most advanced chip manufacturer on the planet — the company that almost every phone, every computer, and every AI chip depends on.

And at the very beginning of it all was a decision made by a fifty-four-year-old man.

**Sometimes the most important move in life is leaving the wrong position; a homecomer's gamble — what he staked was not the rest of his life, but the future of an industry.**

## Knowledge Card · Historical Background of This Episode

(Verified public facts, for the listener's reference)

1. **Leaving TI and General Instrument**: In 1983 Chang resigned from Texas Instruments after 25 years; from 1984 to 1985 he served as president and COO of General Instrument, moved to New York, and was asked to resign within a year over a clash of visions with the CEO.
2. **Taiwan Courts Chang**: As early as 1982, while he was still at TI, Taiwan invited him to head ITRI, but no arrangement was reached; in 1985, after he left General Instrument, he was invited again and accepted, becoming president of the Industrial Technology Research Institute (ITRI).
3. **ITRI and the Hsinchu Science Park**: ITRI was founded in 1973; in 1976 it licensed a 7-micron process from RCA to build its first line; the Hsinchu Science Park was established in 1980 under K.T. Li's leadership.
4. **TSMC and the Pure-Play Foundry Model**: Facing three chip companies' requests to build fabs, Chang proposed a single "common fab" — one that designs nothing, competes with no customer, and only manufactures other people's chips; planning began in 1986, and on February 21, 1987, the world's first dedicated foundry was officially established.
5. **TSMC's Capital Structure**: The government (National Development Fund) held roughly 48%, Philips roughly 27.5%, and the rest was private; Philips invested about $40 million, the government about $70 million, and private investors about $35 million.
6. **The U.S.–Japan Semiconductor Trade War**: In 1986 the U.S.–Japan Semiconductor Agreement committed Japan to improving market access and halting dumping, and let the U.S. set minimum prices on Japanese chips; it ran until March 1991.
7. **K.T. Li and the "Godfather of Technology"**: Li Kwoh-ting (K.T. Li) has been called the "father of Taiwan's economic miracle"; he led the founding of the Hsinchu Science Park and pushed TSMC's creation from start to finish ("Without K.T. Li, there would be no TSMC").
