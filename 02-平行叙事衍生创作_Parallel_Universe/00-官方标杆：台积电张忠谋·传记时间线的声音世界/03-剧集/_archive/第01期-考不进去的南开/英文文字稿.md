## Opening · The Hook

[SFX: the clatter of mahjong tiles, women's laughter, fading low]

Chongqing, 1943. A drawing room. Several ladies are playing mahjong.

A boy stands near the door, and by accident, he overhears his mother talking to the wife of a prominent official.

The lady asks about the boy's schooling. His mother says he hopes to enter the best middle school in the city—Nankai.

The lady sets down her tiles, looks at his mother, and says, flatly:

"He won't get in. You'd be better off finding connections."

[Narrator]

The words hit him like a bucket of cold water.

The boy knew his family's situation too well—they were refugees in Chongqing, strangers in a strange city. What connections could they possibly call on?

In that moment, standing by the door, his heart sank.

But that one sentence would force a boy into the toughest, proudest fight of his young life.

## Act I · The Problem in the Mountain City

[Narrator]

In 1943, Chongqing was China's wartime capital—the great rear area of the nation.

A mountain city that had held fewer than 400,000 people was suddenly flooded with government agencies, factories, schools, and refugees. Around Shapingba, schools clustered together and the district became the famous wartime "cultural zone." And the most famous school in it was Nankai Middle School.

Nankai was the creation of the great educator Zhang Boling. Before the war, he had run schools in Tianjin. When Tianjin fell, Nankai University was forced inland, merging with Peking and Tsinghua universities in Kunming to form the famed Southwest Associated University. Zhang Boling himself, meanwhile, opened a new Nankai Middle School in Chongqing, on the Shapingba hills—it began classes in 1936.

How hard was it to get in?

Every year, admission slots were few, and applicants stormed the gates. A refugee boy, newly arrived, with no background and no connections—the odds were close to zero.

The lady's words weren't cruelty. They were the most realistic judgment the era had to offer.

[Narrator · slower]

But the boy's mother refused to give up.

She learned that Nankai ran a summer program every year—and students who performed exceptionally could be admitted directly.

So she made a decision: the boy would attend the summer session.

Review his studies, yes. And also—take a gamble.

## Act II · The Summer Session and a Comeback

[Narrator]

The boy understood this was his only chance.

In occupied Hong Kong, he had finished barely half a semester of middle school. War had swallowed the better part of a year. Now he was expected to enter the second year directly, competing with classmates who had never stopped studying. On paper, he was behind.

But the boy had one thing others didn't: he truly wanted to study.

In that summer session, he threw everything into it. Classes by day, review by night, clawing his way back through every lesson he had missed.

[Narrator]

And then came the result he hadn't dared to hope for.

When the summer session ended, his grades were exceptional. The school recommended him directly for formal admission as a second-year student.

The news reached home, and his parents were overjoyed. Especially his mother—in an age that prized face, she could finally hold her head up among the ladies at the mahjong table.

The lady who said "he won't get in" probably never guessed that her words had become the boy's best motivation.

## Act III · Days and Nights on Shapingba Hill

[Narrator]

Getting into Nankai didn't make life easy.

The school required all students to live on campus. The dormitory was one vast hall: a long corridor lined on both sides with rows of double-decker bunks. Eight beds formed a cubicle, open to the corridor, separated from its neighbors only by low partition walls.

The dorm's most famous residents were bedbugs. Clean bedding brought in would be crawling with them within days. The first bites kept him awake whole nights; in time, he got used to them—a few red welts each morning, and he slept soundly anyway.

The food was no better. Wartime Chongqing meant coarse, yellowish rice. Meals were mostly vegetarian, with one weekly "feast day" when a plate of braised pork appeared on the table. Sichuan pickled mustard tuber was the one constant. Sometimes the food ran out before the hunger did, and he'd pour soy sauce over rice and keep eating.

[Narrator]

But hardship aside, the boy was, by his own later account, genuinely happy.

This was his first time living away from home, his first taste of communal life, his first steps out from under his parents' shelter. The school ran on strict discipline: reveille at six, classes at eight, compulsory study from seven to nine in the evening, lights out at nine-thirty. Before exams, bold students would secretly light candles in the dorm and pull all-nighters—against the rules, and punishable if caught.

He would later say that the times he learned fastest, felt happiest, and made the most friends were precisely the years spent in that dormitory.

## Act IV · A Boy and a Principal

[Narrator]

Nankai gave the boy more than knowledge.

In his third year, he and a few classmates started a wall newspaper called the *Jian Bao*—named for the line from the *Book of Changes*: "As Heaven moves with vigor, so the gentleman strives ceaselessly to strengthen himself." He ran the literary page and wrote a piece of his own every week.

To run that paper, he twice called on the school's principal, Zhang Boling.

Here was a man past sixty—the founder of the Nankai school system, chairman of the People's Political Council, treated with marked courtesy even by Chiang Kai-shek. Yet face to face with a boy in his early teens, he showed not a trace of hauteur. He talked patiently, tirelessly, like a kindly grandfather.

[Narrator · with feeling]

Many years later, that boy—now an old man himself—was running the world's largest semiconductor company.

One day, he set out to write the operating philosophy for his company. He labored for days and produced ten principles, proud of them as the distilled product of decades of thought and experience. Then, by chance, he came across the Nankai motto again—eight characters: "Be public-spirited and capable; make new progress every day."

He stopped short.

His ten principles, in essence, said nothing more than those eight characters.

## Epilogue · The Parallel View

[Narrator · quiet, slow]

Let's go back to the question: how does a boy with no connections get through the door?

The answer lies in that summer session.

In the lady's worldview, doors are opened by connections. In the boy's worldview, doors are opened by competence.

Decades later, when he founded TSMC, he set down the company's core values in four words: Integrity, Commitment, Innovation, and Customer Trust. The world came to know them by the acronym ICIC.

The "public-spirited" of "public-spirited and capable" is integrity—responsibility, a promise to customers and society. The "new progress every day" is innovation—never standing still. What a refugee boy learned over soy sauce and rice in a Nankai dormitory grew, in the end, into a company that changed the world.

This isn't some mystical twist of fate. It's a plain truth:

**Connections may hand you a ticket. Only competence keeps you in the game.**

The lady who said "he won't get in" probably never imagined her words stopped no one. They simply made a boy carry the motto "public-spirited and capable" with him for the rest of his life.

## Historical Notes · Verified Background

(Public facts cross-checked during research)

1. **Chongqing Nankai Middle School**: Founded by Zhang Boling in 1936 on Shapingba Hill in Chongqing, one of the Nankai school system. During the war, the Shapingba area became the renowned wartime "cultural zone" with many schools.

2. **Zhang Boling (1876–1951)**: Founder of the Nankai schools. During the war, Nankai University merged with Peking and Tsinghua universities in Kunming to form the Southwest Associated University, while Zhang Boling continued Nankai Middle School in Chongqing.

3. **The Motto**: "Be public-spirited and capable; make new progress every day" (允公允能，日新月异)—the Nankai motto, emphasizing public spirit, competence, and constant renewal.

4. **TSMC Core Values (ICIC)**: Integrity, Commitment, Innovation, Customer Trust—according to public reporting, the core values established by Morris Chang for TSMC.

5. **Wartime Student Life**: Boarding, communal living, and coarse fare (yellow rice, mostly vegetarian, one weekly meat day) were the common lot of students in China's wartime interior.
