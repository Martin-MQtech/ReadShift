## Opening · The Hook

[SFX: a foghorn on the Huangpu River, a newsboy's distant cries, fading low]

Shanghai, 1948.

In the dining room of a banker's house, dinner is just over. A seventeen-year-old boy sets down his chopsticks and tells his father what has been on his mind—he wants to be a writer.

His father doesn't get angry. He doesn't laugh at him. He sets down his bowl, a faint smile on his face, and says, very casually:

"Writing is a hard way to make a living. If you become a writer, you might starve."

[Narrator]

The words were light—as ordinary as small talk.

But the boy understood this wasn't a passing remark. It was a judgment delivered by a man who had lived through half a century of upheaval, using every scrap of his own experience.

Years later, recalling that night, the boy would say that he had absolute respect for his father's judgment.

—And so, his dream of becoming a writer faded.

But did it ever truly die?

## Act I · A Literary Boy on the Shanghai Waterfront

[Narrator]

Let's go back to the beginning.

In 1947, the boy came to Shanghai with his parents. Midway through the school year, he enrolled at the high school attached to St. John's University—a beautiful campus set behind Jessfield Park. Six months later, he passed the entrance exam and transferred into the second year of Nanyang Model High School.

Nanyang Model was one of the finest schools in Shanghai, renowned above all for mathematics and the sciences. Most of its graduates went on to Chiao Tung University—a school so respected it was sometimes called "China's MIT." People half-jokingly called Nanyang Model "the Chiao Tung prep school," and the school didn't mind one bit.

But this boy, studying in a school famous for math, carried literature in his heart.

His love of Chinese letters went back years. As a child, frail and poor at sports, he spent long hours alone at home, reading a set of books called the Children's Library. Then, during his two years at Nankai in Chongqing, a teacher of Chinese had guided him through classical texts and modern prose. He wrote for the school wall paper, submitted pieces to other student magazines, and grew to love writing more and more.

By the time he reached Shanghai, that love had ripened. He wrestled, half-comprehending, with the classics—Laozi, Han Feizi, Xunzi, the Book of Songs, the Records of the Grand Historian—while devouring modern writers by the armful: Lu Xun, Hu Shi, Xu Zhimo, Mao Dun, Ba Jin, Bing Xin, Cao Yu... and Xu Xu.

Xu Xu was his favorite. He loved the mood of his fiction—"a light melancholy, a faint sorrow."

At the time, Xu Xu's novel *The Soughing Wind* had just appeared. The boy read it at least three times.

[Narrator · slower]

A seventeen-year-old in a math-obsessed school, quietly devoted to a novelist famous for atmosphere, rereading *The Soughing Wind* three times—somewhere in that boy, literature had planted a seed.

## Act II · "You'll Starve" Across the Dinner Table

[Narrator]

The seed had a name: the dream of being a writer.

A boy of sixteen or seventeen, a few years of reading behind him, a few essays written for a student audience—and he seriously wondered: could I become a writer someday?

Looking back, it was a naive ambition. He had read so few books. But in the honest, half-innocent heart of that boy, it was a sincere wish.

[Narrator · dialogue]

Fortunately, his father woke him from the dream quickly—and skillfully.

Of course his father knew the boy had no idea how far he was from being a real writer. But he didn't say that. He didn't scold, didn't lecture. He simply said, with worldly ease, a faint smile on his face:

"Writing is a hard way to make a living. If you become a writer, you might starve."

[Narrator]

And the boy had absolute respect for his father's judgment.

His father was a Ningbo man who had spent his life in banking. He had seen too much. He knew writing couldn't feed a family—not out of prejudice, but out of the hard lessons his era had taught every adult.

The boy didn't argue. He didn't rebel. He simply let the ambition fade.

## Act III · The Gold Yuan: The Era's Own "You'll Starve"

[Narrator]

The weight behind those words, the boy would witness with his own eyes soon enough.

In the second half of 1948, the war turned sharply for the worse. Hyperinflation had been festering for years—and as the war soured, prices rose to desperate, almost unbelievable levels.

That summer, the government reformed the currency. The fabi was replaced by the gold yuan, and the public was forbidden to hold gold or foreign currency. Everyone's gold and dollars had to be exchanged for gold yuan within a strict deadline.

Nobody trusted the gold yuan. If money could turn to paper overnight, what could you do? Buy things. So Shanghai poured into real estate—and property prices in the city doubled and redoubled within days.

[Narrator · with feeling]

The frenzy lasted barely two months.

Then the war deteriorated further. Armies closed in on Xuzhou; the great battle was about to break. Panic seized Shanghai. Real estate, which had soared within days, collapsed back below its old prices.

That bitterly ironic tragedy ruined countless families.

[Narrator · quiet]

Now look back at that dinner table.

The father said "you'll starve" on the eve of that madness.

A man who had spent his whole career in banking, watching his colleagues, his friends, his neighbors lose everything overnight to inflation—he knew better than anyone what a young man who lived by his pen would be walking into.

"You'll starve" was not conservatism. It was not discouragement. It was the era itself, in its cruellest form, speaking through a father's mouth.

## Act IV · Years Later, a Book in an Old Shop

[Narrator · with feeling, slower]

The dream was pressed down.

But it never died—it simply found another way to live.

Decades later, the boy was an old man, running the world's largest semiconductor company. One day, passing an old bookshop in a small town in central Taiwan, he spotted a copy of *The Soughing Wind* on the shelf.

He bought it on the spot. Back at his hotel, he read through the night, reliving the taste of reading it as a boy.

Xu Xu was long gone, and his fame had quietly faded. The old man still believed Xu Xu deserved to be remembered as a far greater writer than he was.

[Narrator · with feeling]

And the dream that had been pressed down?

The old man would later write that his boyhood dream of becoming a writer was now only a memory. He asked himself: can I still write a long work in Chinese?

And then he did it. Two years, hundreds of hours, and word by word, an autobiography of hundreds of thousands of characters.

—The dream that had been put down across a dinner table woke up on its own, when he was past sixty.

## Epilogue · The Parallel View

[Narrator · quiet, slow]

Let's return to the question: dream or reality—which deserves your trust?

The boy trusted his father, gave up the writing life, and walked into a world he had never imagined—semiconductors.

But what if the boy had become a writer after all? Perhaps China would have gained an ordinary man of letters, and the world would have lost the founder of a semiconductor empire. Fate offered him no second choice—but it gave him a life in which the dream stayed hidden in his heart and traveled with him across the world.

His father protected him with a sentence, steering him away from a road that might have led to hunger. And the dream itself never really went out—it became the eloquence of his speeches, the depth of his autobiography, an old man's lifelong devotion to the Chinese language and to literature.

Perhaps this is the truth about dreams and reality:

**His father taught him how to survive in this world; the dream he was told to put down taught him how to remember who he was.**

## Historical Notes · Verified Background

(Public facts cross-checked during research)

1. **Nanyang Model High School**: A celebrated Shanghai school whose roots trace to 1901, with deep ties to the Nanyang Public School and Chiao Tung University. Morris Chang transferred into its second year in 1948.
2. **Xu Xu (1908–1980)**: Modern Chinese writer whose novel *The Soughing Wind* (*Feng Xiaoxiao*), published in 1943, was one of the most popular novels of the war years, known for its mood of "a light melancholy, a faint sorrow."
3. **The Gold Yuan Reform of 1948**: In August 1948 the Nationalist government replaced the fabi with the gold yuan and forced the surrender of private gold and foreign currency; lacking reserves and over-issued, the currency collapsed within months, wiping out urban middle-class savings and marking a turning point in the era's economic breakdown.
4. **The Huaihai Campaign (徐蚌会战)**: November 6, 1948 – January 10, 1949; one of the decisive campaigns of the Chinese Civil War, fought across the Xuzhou–Bengbu region.
5. **Shanghai in 1948**: After the currency reform, prices soared, real estate first boomed then crashed, and anxiety gripped the city—glitter and crisis side by side on the eve of the old order's collapse.
