# Morris Chang's Parallel World · Episode 05: "Watching the Rebellion from Afar"

> AIGC Parallel Biography — "The Parallel World of the TSMC Founder's Timeline"

## Opening Hook

[SFX: an old typewriter clattering, a telephone ringing in the distance, footsteps fading]

Palo Alto, California. Autumn, 1957.

Outside a semiconductor laboratory, eight young men walk out the door, carrying cardboard boxes.

Their boss is William Shockley — a Nobel laureate, one of the three inventors of the transistor. Months earlier, he had been treated like a saint, the most celebrated figure in the semiconductor world.

And these eight men are doing something that, at the time, looked like sheer ingratitude:

They are resigning. Together.

[Main narrator]

The news rippled through the industry.

Some called them ungrateful. Some laughed at them for throwing away their futures. One headline gave the eight men a name dripping with sarcasm —

"The Traitorous Eight."

From that moment, Silicon Valley had its true first chapter.

Three thousand kilometers away, in Dallas, a twenty-six-year-old man had just walked into a company called Texas Instruments.

His name is Morris Chang.

He could not have known that this rebellion, three thousand kilometers away, would become one of the most important reference points of his entire career.

---

## Act One · The Arrogance of a Genius

[Main narrator]

To understand the rebellion, we must start with the man it rebelled against.

William Shockley was one of the greatest physicists of the twentieth century. In 1947, at Bell Labs, he and Bardeen and Brattain had invented the transistor; in 1956, he won the Nobel Prize in Physics.

At the height of his fame, Shockley made a bold decision: leave Bell Labs, return to his native California, and start his own semiconductor company.

He was going to build the future. Silicon.

[Main narrator, slower]

Shockley did not lack genius. He lacked something else.

He recruited some of the best young Ph.D.s in America — among them two men who would one day change the world: Robert Noyce, and Gordon Moore.

It was an era when the semiconductor industry was just being born. The transistor was barely ten years old; silicon was about to replace germanium as the new king of materials. Shockley Laboratories was regarded as one of the most promising places in America.

But Shockley was a terrible boss.

He was brilliant, and arrogant; clever, and paranoid. He suspected his colleagues of stealing his research. He demanded lie-detector tests. He flew into rages over trivialities. Once, over a trivial issue, he humiliated an engineer in front of the whole staff. The most talented people in the lab grew more and more disheartened.

The young men gathered privately, more and more often, and asked each other the same questions: Why are we staying? Why can't we do it ourselves?

It was the oldest story in the world — genius at the top, frustration at the bottom. But this time, the frustrated men at the bottom were, in their own right, among the most gifted engineers of their generation. They were not ordinary employees. They were future founders.

[Main narrator]

Finally, in 1957, patience ran out.

Eight young men — Noyce, Moore, and six other engineers — decided to leave.

And they had an even crazier idea: not to join another company, but to start their own.

But starting a company takes money. And they were mostly young engineers with little in their pockets.

Just then, a young man appeared. His name was Arthur Rock, a securities analyst at an investment bank in New York.

Rock heard their plan and thought it had promise. He carried their business plan around New York, knocking on the doors of company after company.

He was turned away everywhere.

That was 1957. On Wall Street, the concept of "venture capital" did not yet exist. Nobody wanted to bet money on a group of recently-resigned young engineers, in an industry that did not even have a product yet. The transistor was real, but the market for it was still a guess.

Rock later recalled that he was nearly thrown out of some of those offices.

But he kept going. He believed in the men more than in the product — a conviction that would define his entire career.

[Main narrator, slower]

Finally, Rock found a key figure — Sherman Fairchild.

Fairchild was a wealthy industrialist. The camera-and-instrument company his father had founded was a giant of the aviation industry.

Fairchild listened to Rock's pitch and made a decision that changed history: invest one and a half million dollars to back these eight young men in a brand-new semiconductor company.

In exchange, his company and the investment bank Hayden Stone received a share of the new firm — Hayden Stone took twenty percent.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

Remember this date: 1957.

Before it, Silicon Valley was still a land of orchards, famous for its apricot and cherry trees. After it, the world's technology map would be redrawn around a name — Fairchild.

And the eight men had earned themselves a nickname that stuck:

"The Traitorous Eight."

It was meant as an insult, and they wore it without shame. Years later, Noyce would recall: we were not traitors. We simply did not want to waste our talents anymore.

## Act Two · The First Cry of Silicon Valley

[Main narrator]

Fairchild Semiconductor was born in Palo Alto, just twelve blocks from Shockley's laboratory.

Two former workplaces, facing each other across the street. One on the left, one on the right.

Fairchild's beginnings were humble. No proper office building, no luxurious equipment — just a group of young men and a storehouse of pent-up energy. They rented space and set to work with their own hands, building their own furnaces, improvising their own equipment.

It did not look like the birth of an empire. It looked like a garage project. But empires, in that era, often began in garages.

[Main narrator, slower]

But in just a few years, that group of young men did one world-changing thing after another.

Noyce — called "the natural leader" by his colleagues — led Fairchild to build the silicon integrated circuit: the ancestor of nearly every chip that followed.

A year earlier, Jack Kilby at Texas Instruments had already built the first integrated circuit out of germanium — but it was a hybrid, with tiny gold wires connecting the parts by hand. Fragile. Hard to mass-produce.

Noyce's approach was different: he put the entire circuit inside a single piece of silicon. Using a method called the planar process, he etched transistors, resistors, and wires onto the same chip, all in one step.

— That was the chip that could actually be mass-produced.

Fairchild invented the planar process, invented the path to commercializing the integrated circuit, and became the "West Point" of the semiconductor industry.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

Here is a statistic that shows Fairchild's status:

Nearly every famous semiconductor company that followed in Silicon Valley — Intel, AMD, National Semiconductor — was founded by men who came from Fairchild, or were connected to it by blood and by memory.

People gave these men a name: the "Fairchildren."

And all of it traces back to 1957 — to eight men who dared to leave.

Years later, someone wrote of Fairchild: it was the seed company of Silicon Valley. What it scattered was not seeds, but generation after generation of people who changed the world.

## Act Three · The Observer in Dallas

[Main narrator]

Now let us cut to Dallas, three thousand kilometers away.

In 1958, Morris Chang left Sylvania and joined Texas Instruments.

What kind of company was it?

If you had to sum it up in one phrase: a company that bet everything on the space race.

In September 1958, shortly after Chang joined, a TI engineer named Jack Kilby built the first integrated circuit in history in the company's laboratory. A year later, Robert Noyce at Fairchild produced an improved version using the silicon planar process.

The fight over who invented the integrated circuit would rage for years. But either way, Texas Instruments stood at the very front of the revolution.

[Main narrator, slower]

And Chang stood at the eye of the storm.

Dallas in 1958 was a different world from California. Silicon Valley was rebelling; Texas Instruments was expanding. No orchards here — just endless plains, and a company with boundless ambition.

He took over a production line making transistors for IBM. The yield was almost zero.

It was the most primitive era of the semiconductor industry. No precision equipment, no automated lines — everything depended on an engineer's experience and intuition, on "guessing" — guessing the temperature, the time, the materials. At IBM, the best they could manage on a pilot line was a ten percent yield. At TI, Chang's line was producing almost nothing but rejects.

The supervisor was worried. The operators were worried. Everybody was worried. But Chang was not, because he knew that ten percent was at least possible.

He put every bit of semiconductor knowledge he had taught himself over three years at Sylvania to work. He adjusted the process parameters again and again — temperature, time, alloys, diffusion materials — and after about four months, the yield climbed from near zero to more than twenty percent.

That night, he was too excited to sleep.

IBM thought he had been lucky. But when IBM engineers came to talk to him, he explained the theories behind his experiments, and why his process worked. There was no luck in it. There was knowledge.

Suddenly, even the president of Texas Instruments knew his name.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

This story deserves a moment's thought.

Chang would spend decades in the semiconductor industry, and he repeated one word over and over: yield.

Yield — out of a hundred chips you make, how many actually work.

To others, it was the most unglamorous metric in the factory. But Chang understood from day one: the battle in manufacturing is never decided in the laboratory. It is decided on the production line.

At Texas Instruments, Kilby invented the integrated circuit in the lab and won the Nobel Prize. Chang solved the yield problem on the production line — and won the market.

There is only one Nobel Prize. But the market feeds an entire industry.

Kilby's achievement made him a legend. Chang's achievement made him a manager — it was his first step onto the management track, and it propelled him, twenty-plus engineers in his department, toward the executive suite.

That plain conviction — that the battle is won on the production line — thirty years later, would become the soul of TSMC.

## Act Four · The Second Rebellion

[Main narrator]

Back to the West Coast.

Fairchild Semiconductor took off fast. But it had problems of its own.

Its parent company, Fairchild Camera and Instrument, was an old-style industrial firm. It treated the semiconductor division like a cash cow, siphoning off the profits. And the founders — brilliant as they were — owned almost no stock in the company they had built.

Gifted men discovered they were working for someone else.

[Main narrator, slower]

History began to repeat itself.

In 1968, Noyce and Moore — two of the original Traitorous Eight — decided to leave again.

Fairchild had brought them fame, but not fortune. They had little equity, and the parent company kept taking their best projects away.

They had had enough.

They were going to start another company.

This time, they went to the same man: Arthur Rock.

By then, Rock was already one of the pioneers of American venture capital. Ten years earlier, he had pushed the Traitorous Eight onto the road of entrepreneurship. Ten years later, he would push again.

Noyce called Rock. He said: we want to do it again.

[Main narrator]

On July 18, 1968, Intel Corporation was founded.

Noyce and Moore each put in roughly $250,000 of their own money. Rock became chairman and raised $2.5 million in convertible debentures from private investors.

Intel — the name that would one day rule the world of computer chips — was born.

Among its first employees was a young Hungarian-American engineer named Andy Grove. For decades to come, Noyce, Moore, and Grove would be known as Intel's "three musketeers."

Noyce provided the vision and the charisma. Moore provided the science — including, in 1965, a famous observation about how the number of transistors on a chip doubles every couple of years, which the world would come to know as Moore's Law. Grove provided the relentless operational drive.

Three very different men, one company, one era.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

And this second founding differed from the first in one essential way:

This time, the founders truly owned their company.

The principle Rock insisted on became the bible of Silicon Valley: entrepreneurs must own equity in their company. You take the biggest risk; you should share the biggest reward.

From Fairchild's "sewing for others" to Intel's "building for yourself" — the equity revolution of Silicon Valley was complete.

History would prove that this revolution transformed Silicon Valley from a gathering of geniuses into a factory of dreams.

Because only when you truly own your company do you stake your life on it. And only when your life is at stake do you do your best work.

It is a lesson Chang absorbed from a distance — and one he would apply, decades later, when he built TSMC with a structure that made the people who built it also own it.

## Epilogue · A Parallel View

[Main narrator, quiet, slower]

In 1972, Morris Chang was promoted to group vice president of Texas Instruments, in charge of its worldwide semiconductor business.

By then, Intel was four years old and racing down the road of the microprocessor. In 1971, it had introduced the 4004 — the world's first commercial microprocessor: a chip the size of a fingernail, holding an entire computer's central processor inside it.

From that year on, Chang's main rival was Intel.

One was the man who had once watched the rebellion from three thousand kilometers away. The other was the hardest fruit that rebellion ever bore. They would meet, at last, at the summit of the semiconductor world.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

Let us stretch the timeline a little further.

1957: eight men in Palo Alto defect from Shockley — and Chang watches from Dallas, three thousand kilometers away.

1968: Noyce and Moore walk out again, founding Intel — and Chang is still in Dallas, busy making TI's integrated circuit business the largest in the world.

1998: he is sixty-seven years old, in Taiwan. The company he founded, TSMC, is eleven years old.

[Main narrator, quiet]

The rebellion he watched from half a continent away later became his own script.

Only this time, he was no longer an observer.

He carried the faith of "yield" back to Taiwan. He turned the conviction that "manufacturing is won on the production line" into a company.

That company does not design chips. It does not brand them. It does one thing: manufacture chips for others — and make them better than anyone else can.

— Just as, fifty years earlier, the first cry of Silicon Valley began with eight men who dared to leave.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

People often say that Silicon Valley began with technology.

It did not.

Technology mattered — but if it were only about technology, those eight young men in 1957 could have stayed quietly beside Shockley, waiting for a share of the Nobel laureate's reflected glory.

They did not.

Silicon Valley began with the courage of eight men to leave — with the courage of Noyce and Moore to leave a second time — with the courage of countless people to stake their lives on an idea.

Chang watched all of this from Dallas, and learned the lesson:

**True revolution never begins with joining. It begins with leaving.**

And his own "leaving" would come in 1985 — when he left Texas Instruments and returned to Taiwan.

That time, he was no longer an observer.

**The rebellion he watched from half a continent away later became his own script — only his stage was in Taiwan.**

## Knowledge Card · Historical Background of This Episode

(Verified public facts, for the listener's reference)

1. **The Traitorous Eight (1957)**: Eight young engineers collectively resigned from Shockley Semiconductor Laboratory and founded Fairchild Semiconductor. The media dubbed them the "Traitorous Eight."
2. **Fairchild's Financing (1957)**: Securities analyst Arthur Rock brokered the deal; industrialist Sherman Fairchild invested $1.5 million to back the venture; the investment bank Hayden Stone received a 20 percent stake.
3. **Fairchild's Legacy**: Fairchild invented the silicon integrated circuit and the planar process, becoming the "West Point" of semiconductors — the founders of Intel, AMD, National Semiconductor and others trace their lineage to it (the "Fairchildren").
4. **The Founding of Intel (July 18, 1968)**: Noyce and Moore left Fairchild to found Intel, each investing roughly $250,000; Arthur Rock served as chairman and raised $2.5 million in convertible debentures; Andy Grove was among the earliest core employees (the "three musketeers").
5. **The Equity Revolution**: At Fairchild the founders held almost no equity; at Intel, Rock insisted the founders own their company — a pivotal moment in the formation of Silicon Valley's venture capital culture.
6. **Chang's Coordinates in the Same Era**: He joined Texas Instruments in 1958 and raised the yield of an IBM production line from near zero to over 20 percent; in 1972 he became TI's group vice president for worldwide semiconductors, with Intel (which launched the world's first commercial microprocessor, the 4004, in 1971) as his main rival.
