# Morris Chang's Parallel World · Episode 03: "From the Huangpu to the Charles"

> AIGC Parallel Biography — "The Parallel World of the TSMC Founder's Timeline"
> This episode's lens: **An eighteen-year-old boy treats leaving as temporary — and walks a road that has no return.** From the Huangpu River to the Charles River, from Shanghai to Boston, one man's journey across civilizations begins.
> Target length: 20 minutes (English ~3,300 words)
> Original creative work based on public record; no text from any copyrighted book is reproduced.

---

## Opening Hook

[SFX: the sound of waves, a ship's horn, fading to a hush]

Shanghai, summer 1949. The Bund, on the Huangpu River.

An eighteen-year-old boy stands in the crowd on the dock. His parents are beside him; behind him lies the city where he was born, where he grew up, and which he has already fled twice.

He is leaving. For America. For college.

This departure was, by his count, the latest in a long line of moves — a boy who had fled war had long since learned how to say goodbye. At five, he moved from Canton to Hong Kong. At eleven, he crossed the front lines to Chongqing. At seventeen, he fled Shanghai back to Hong Kong. Every departure meant upheaval, meant uncertainty.

So this time, he did not think too hard about it.

He simply assumed this was another temporary leave — like all the others. In a year or two, when things settled down, he would come home.

[Main narrator]

He did not know.

He did not know that he, like tens of thousands of intellectuals of his generation, was treating "temporary" as a return date — and walking a road with no return.

On the dock, his mother probably said the old words once more: write to us when you get there. His father said little, only helped hand his luggage up the gangway.

The boy waved.

Years later, he would recall that this was the last true "moving-house goodbye" of his life — every departure after it carried a quality of permanence, though he did not know it at the time.

This episode begins with that farewell: how an eighteen-year-old left the Huangpu River, arrived at Harvard on the banks of the Charles, and, in the space of a single year, made a choice that changed everything.

---

## Act One · Seven Months in Hong Kong

[Main narrator]

Let us rewind a year.

In late 1948, the thunder of the Huaihai Campaign — one of the decisive battles of the civil war — rolled toward Shanghai. Panic gripped the city. For the third time, the boy's family took to the road. Their destination: Hong Kong.

Hong Kong again. At five, fleeing war, his first stop had been Hong Kong. Now, at seventeen, the last stop of his flight was Hong Kong once more.

[Main narrator, slower]

In January 1949, his father finally made it to Hong Kong, and the family was reunited.

The next seven months were a comparatively calm stretch for the family. Postwar Hong Kong was swelling — tens of thousands of refugees were pouring in from the mainland, packing the little island to bursting. The boy studied at a private academy by day, and did what countless families were doing: applied for passports, applied for visas, applied to American universities. Every line at the consulate told the same story: a city of people buying one-way tickets out of history.

Why America?

The reason was brutally practical. The war situation was clear; the universities on the mainland were in turmoil, and Hong Kong had almost none. America, to countless Chinese families, was both a refuge and a new world.

Which school, then? The boy had no idea. That was when one man's advice proved decisive — his uncle, Zhang Sihou.

[Main narrator]

This uncle was the most learned man of his father's generation.

He held a doctorate in electrical engineering from Harvard, and taught as a professor at Northeastern University in Boston. Looking back decades later, this uncle almost single-handedly mapped the boy's route to America.

And his advice was unexpected. He did not steer the boy toward engineering, toward his own field. He recommended Harvard — Harvard's undergraduate program, first year.

Why?

[Main narrator, dialogue]

It took many years for the uncle's reasoning to become known. He said that MIT was a special school, and that his nephew was still an unsettled kid —

"Spend one year figuring out what you want to do."

[Main narrator]

That sentence was a rare kind of patience for the era.

In July 1949, everything was ready. At Kai Tak Airport in Hong Kong, the boy boarded a Pan American flight bound for San Francisco.

It was the dividing line of his life.

## Act Two · Over the Pacific

[SFX: aircraft engines, rising then fading into quiet]

[Main narrator]

From Hong Kong to San Francisco, across the entire Pacific Ocean.

Today, it is hard to imagine what transpacific flight meant in 1949. These were the years of propeller airliners — no jets, no nonstop routes. Planes stopped at island outposts to refuel, flying for dozens of hours to cross the ocean.

An eighteen-year-old boy, on his first flight, leaving Asia for the first time, heading for a country he had only read about.

[Main narrator, slower]

When the plane touched down in San Francisco, he could hardly have imagined —

that this city would become, four years later, the starting point of a life shared with a young woman; that this country would become the stage of his entire career.

He simply followed the crowd, cleared immigration, and boarded a cross-continental train heading east.

Out the window, America looked both familiar and strange: vast midwestern plains, industrial cities bristling with smokestacks, cars streaming along the new highways — a nation booming at a speed that startled the world. He watched the landscape roll past for days, and filed it all away, wordless.

Destination: Cambridge, Massachusetts. The banks of the Charles River.

Harvard.

---

## Act Three · A Moveable Feast

[Main narrator]

In the fall of 1949, the boy reported to Harvard as a freshman.

Harvard's first lesson was not academic. It was life.

His dormitory stood in Harvard Yard, an old three-story building with the bathrooms in the basement. His roommate was an American student named Sinclair. Meals were served in the dining halls, in orderly fashion — and at dinner, you wore a jacket and tie.

[Main narrator, slower]

What truly stunned him were the people his own age.

In his refugee boyhood, his classmates had all carried their own burdens — war, displacement, lost schooling. Harvard's students were different. His dorm-mates included a pianist, an architecture student, a political science major — one who loved to debate where Communist China was heading.

The Harvard dormitory was like a small universe.

[Main narrator]

Music was the other protagonist of that year.

The boy bought a season ticket to the Boston Symphony Orchestra, and heard a world-class performance every week. Pianists Arthur Rubinstein and Vladimir Horowitz, violinist Jascha Heifetz, tenor Nelson Eddy — names he had only heard on records in Shanghai, now standing before him, close enough to see the sweat on their foreheads.

Boston was a cultural capital, and he drank it in. He went to ballet, to the theater, and slowly developed a discerning ear. He had grown up with classical music — a gramophone in Shanghai, records carried through the refugee years. Now the same music was live before him. Decades later, running TSMC, the speakers in his office would still be playing the world from Bach to Mahler.

The theater moved him, too. One play haunted him for days — "Death of a Salesman." The tragic fate of its hero, and the social forces that shaped him, weighed on the boy's chest. Then there was Shaw's "Man and Superman": four famous actors in formal dress, reading their lines, in a packed hall.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

That year, Harvard also handed him an unexpected challenge — swimming.

Harvard required every freshman to master at least one athletic skill and pass a swimming test. He did not know how to swim, so every Saturday he reported to the pool for an hour's lesson.

One by one, his classmates learned, passed, and moved on to sports they actually liked. He, clumsy as ever, struggled. The coach's patience wore thin.

Finally, he swam the hundred meters. The coach exhaled in relief and congratulated him. In the pool, only one classmate remained — still flailing in the water, fighting on.

[Main narrator, quiet]

At the end of the year, his report card read: A in physics, mathematics, and English; B in chemistry and humanities.

Harvard graded on a strict curve — roughly one student in ten earned an A. With three A's and two B's, he stood in the top ten percent of his class.

A boy too clumsy to learn to swim had, in his studies, turned out to be effortlessly good. Perhaps it was fate's hint: the things you are bad at, you can learn slowly; the things you are good at, were already written in your nature.

In a country rebuilding itself on science and engineering, a young man who could master physics, mathematics, and English was holding keys to the future — even if he did not yet know which door they opened.

[Main narrator, slower]

Many years later, the boy was an old man, and in his autobiography he borrowed Hemingway's phrase for Paris —

"A moveable feast."

Wherever he went afterward — MIT, Stanford, Texas Instruments, or back to Taiwan — he said, he carried that feast with him, always.

## Act Four · The World Turns a Page, Far Away

[Main narrator]

Even the most beautiful feast must end.

While the boy listened to symphonies and read Shakespeare on the banks of the Charles, the world on the other side of the Pacific was turning a page.

On October 1, 1949, the People's Republic of China was proclaimed.

In October, Canton and Xiamen fell; in November, Chongqing; in December, Chengdu. That same month, the Nationalist government moved its capital to Taipei.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

The "temporary leave" the boy had believed in was becoming less temporary by the day.

Hong Kong was uneasy. Many feared that it would be next. Letters from home grew heavier with worry each month.

The boy's hope of "returning home after studies" guttered like a lamp in the Pacific wind.

[Main narrator]

And the world around him was being remade, too.

In the summer of 1947, a U.S. Secretary of State had announced, at a Harvard commencement, a plan to rebuild Europe — a plan that would carry his name: the Marshall Plan. More than twelve billion dollars, crossing the ocean to lift a continent out of ruins.

Would the boy, years later, remember that his first year in America had coincided with some of the most consequential events of the century? At eighteen, most of it passed over his head — he was too busy with symphonies, swimming lessons, and report cards. But history, indifferent to attention, kept turning anyway.

Three years before he arrived, in March 1946, Churchill had spoken in Fulton, Missouri, and delivered the line that would define an era — "an iron curtain has descended across the Continent of Europe." The Cold War had begun.

America was becoming, at an unprecedented pace, the center of the free world. The Marshall Plan poured aid into Western Europe. The G.I. Bill sent millions of veterans to college, and helped create the largest middle class in human history.

[Main narrator, slower]

It was an age of contradictions: the East was dividing, the West was rebuilding; people fled across one ocean, while families decorated new homes in the suburbs along the highways.

And standing at the center of it all was an eighteen-year-old Chinese boy. In his hand, a Harvard report card. In his heart, a "moveable feast." Before him, one question —

Where do I go from here?

---

## Act Five · The Choice to Transfer

[Main narrator]

His father's letter arrived first.

The father was a banker, a practical man his whole life. His meaning was clear: literature and history cannot feed you in this era; engineering is the future.

He was not rejecting the feast. He was telling his son: the feast is over. Now think about making a living.

[Main narrator]

The boy had been thinking, too.

In that Harvard year, he had absorbed Western culture like a sponge — literature, art, ideas — made countless American friends, and felt himself part of American society. But when it came to a skill that could support a life, he knew the truth: he had not seriously studied engineering.

And the uncle's original advice had come due — "spend one year figuring out what you want to do."

The year was up.

[Main narrator, dialogue]

The uncle was a liberal in education, believing young people should choose their own paths. The boy asked him: what about mechanical engineering?

The uncle said: Excellent.

[Main narrator]

And that was that.

In the spring of 1950, while still in his second semester at Harvard, the boy applied to transfer to MIT — the Massachusetts Institute of Technology — as a sophomore, in mechanical engineering.

Why mechanical engineering? He admitted later that he knew almost nothing about the different branches of engineering. He simply had a vague idea that engineering meant machines — and surely mechanical engineering covered the broadest ground.

In the summer of 1950, his parents came to America, too. Hong Kong felt too risky; they feared being caught up in the turmoil, so the whole family moved. It was their first time abroad.

In September 1950, the boy reported to MIT as a sophomore in mechanical engineering.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

He could hardly have known what kind of institution he was walking into.

MIT was founded in 1861, long recognized as America's preeminent institute of technology. During World War II, its Radiation Laboratory developed radar for the U.S. military — nearly half of all radar deployed by American forces in that war came out of this one laboratory.

The postwar decades were the zenith of MIT's influence. Its presidents served, formally or informally, as science advisers to presidents of the United States.

In 1950, MIT enrolled some seven or eight thousand students — half undergraduate, half graduate; about ten percent foreign students. Women? About one percent. A few dozen, in the entire campus.

[Main narrator, slower]

Compared with Harvard, MIT's students were harder-working and more reserved.

They were not as dazzling as his Harvard classmates; conversation with them was less sparkling. But they "lived and breathed engineering" — eating, walking, talking, three sentences out of five about their field. The course load was heavier, too: Harvard averaged four courses a semester; MIT required five or six.

And at the lectern stood true masters: Den Hartog in applied mechanics, Shapiro in fluid mechanics, Keenan in thermodynamics — men who could make the hardest material perfectly clear. No question was too trivial for them to answer; if one stumped them, they would say, "Let me think it over and tell you next class" — and they never once failed to return with an answer.

Money was tight, too. His mother had warned him before he left: his father could cover the first year, and after that he would need scholarships and part-time work. At MIT he had a scholarship, but it was not enough. He typed papers for other students — he had learned to type as a child, in the months when occupied Hong Kong had kept him out of school. He did calculation work for professors, hour after hour at a mechanical calculator, for ninety cents an hour.

It was not glamorous. But it was real, and it was his own. The boy told himself: at Harvard, I absorbed culture; in engineering, I have not yet done serious work. Now it is time to make up for it.

At nineteen, he truly began his grind in engineering.

## Epilogue · A Parallel View

[Main narrator, quiet, slower]

Let us pull the camera back and look at these three years once more.

In 1948, at seventeen, he said goodbye to Shanghai on the banks of the Huangpu. In 1949, at eighteen, he said goodbye to Hong Kong at Kai Tak. In 1950, at nineteen, he said goodbye to Harvard on the banks of the Charles.

Every goodbye, he thought, was temporary.

But the era was turning countless "temporaries" into "forevers."

[Main narrator, with feeling]

Around 1949, a vast diaspora of intellectuals made their choices: some stayed on the mainland, some crossed the strait to Taiwan, and some — like him — crossed the ocean.

History books call it the dispersal of the intelligentsia. In his own life, it had a more ordinary name: moving house.

Only this time, the destination was no longer a city in China.

His life would still pass through Texas Instruments, Stanford, and back to Taiwan. But the true watershed was drawn in these three years: he left the civilization that raised him, entered another — and never truly went back to live.

Years later, a man of great achievement, he occasionally returned to the old places on the mainland. The rivers and mountains were the same; the old friends were gone. Standing by the Huangpu, he wondered: if he had not left in 1949, would his life have been another story?

No one can answer that question. History has no ifs — only choices, and the long bearing of their consequences.

And yet the boy who left was still there, in the old man: the one who had learned, across every move, that the world could be re-learned — that a new language, a new craft, a new civilization were all just another set of lessons, if you were willing to start from zero. That lesson, polished in a Harvard dormitory and sharpened in an MIT classroom, was in the end the only luggage he carried home.

[Main narrator, quiet]

From the Huangpu to the Charles is not a journey. It is a dividing line.

The company he would one day found distilled the whole passage — from East to West, from war to peace, from idealism to pragmatism — into a single kind of commercial spirit: the ability to start over in a strange world, to learn from zero, and to rebuild oneself.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

And the starting point of it all was that day in the summer of 1949, on the banks of the Huangpu, an eighteen-year-old boy waving goodbye to his parents.

He thought it was just a move.

He did not know that he, like so many intellectuals of his generation, treated leaving as temporary — and walked a road with no return.

**He did not know that the dock that day was his last photograph with the old world.**

## Knowledge Card · Historical Background of This Episode

(Verified public facts, for the listener's reference)

1. **China in 1949**: The People's Republic of China was proclaimed on October 1, 1949; Canton, Xiamen, Chongqing, and Chengdu changed hands between October and December, and the Nationalist government moved its capital to Taipei that December. (Per international English-language consensus: U.S. State Department Office of the Historian, English Wikipedia.)
2. **The Iron Curtain Speech**: On March 5, 1946, Winston Churchill spoke at Fulton, Missouri, declaring that "an iron curtain has descended across the Continent of Europe" — widely seen as a marker of the start of the Cold War.
3. **The Marshall Plan**: Announced in 1947 and enacted in 1948 as the European Recovery Program, it delivered more than $12 billion in aid to Western Europe by 1951, profoundly shaping the postwar economies of Europe and America.
4. **The G.I. Bill (1944)**: The U.S. Servicemen's Readjustment Act, which funded college tuition, low-interest home loans, and unemployment relief for veterans — a key engine of postwar middle-class expansion and consumer prosperity.
5. **The Harvard Redbook (1945)**: "General Education in a Free Society," the Harvard report championed by President James B. Conant, which laid the intellectual foundation of Harvard's liberal arts curriculum — the very education Morris Chang received in his freshman year.
6. **MIT's Radiation Laboratory (1940–45)**: The wartime lab that built nearly half of all U.S. military radar, developed more than 100 radar systems worth some $1.5 billion, and helped establish MIT's postwar ascendancy in science and technology.
7. **Pan American's transpacific route in 1949**: Flying from Kai Tak, Hong Kong to San Francisco required multiple stops and dozens of hours in the air; Morris Chang took this route to America in July 1949.
