## Opening Hook

[SFX: the low hum of a cleanroom, the whir of machinery, fading down]

Hsinchu, Taiwan. 1987.

A new company opens its doors. It has not designed a single chip of its own, it has no advanced technology to speak of, and even its production line is an old line carried over from ITRI.

The whole semiconductor industry is waiting to see how soon it will collapse.

[Main narrator]

Because in everyone's common sense, a chip company must design its own chips, and then manufacture its own chips. Design and manufacturing were two sides of the same coin; neither could exist without the other.

And yet this company did only half of it.

It designed nothing. It only manufactured — and it only manufactured other people's chips.

[Main narrator, slower]

Years later, the world would give this model a name: the foundry.

But back then, almost everyone thought it was a joke.

[Main narrator]

The company was TSMC. Its founder was Morris Chang.

And this episode is the story of how that "joke" carved out an entirely new industry in less than a decade — and redrew the map of chips for the whole world.

---

## Act One · The Bet That Was Laughed At

[Main narrator]

In February 1987, TSMC was formally established.

The rule Chang set for it was simple to the point of stubbornness: this company would never design its own chips, never sell its own products, and never compete with its own customers.

It would do exactly one thing — manufacture the chips that other people had designed.

[Main narrator, slower]

In the semiconductor industry of that time, this was close to heresy.

The giants of the day — Intel, Texas Instruments, Motorola — all walked the same road: design, manufacturing, and sales, all held tightly in one pair of hands. They treated their fabs as secret weapons. Who would hand something so valuable over to strangers?

[Main narrator]

So when Chang proposed "the foundry," he was met with laughter from all sides.

[Main narrator, slower]

It was an age of "do everything yourself." A chip company's pipeline ran from the drawing board, through the fab, to the customer's door, in one unbroken line. Whoever held that whole line in his hands was the king.

In the eyes of the industry, a company that only made chips but designed none was at best a small workshop serving the giants. It had no soul, no products of its own — and therefore, no future.

[Main narrator]

Some predicted that no one would ever hand a hard-won chip design to a small, unknown factory. Others ran the numbers and said a fab cost a fortune — working as a hired hand for others, you would never earn it back.

Even the local industry in Taiwan had its doubts.

[Main narrator, slower]

But in Chang's mind, a different picture was forming.

He knew this industry's cost structure inside out. He knew the cost of a fab was climbing year after year. He also knew that only by pushing output high enough could costs fall — the very "learning curve" with which he had won his battles at Texas Instruments.

And he knew one more thing that others paid little attention to: Taiwan's factories achieved unusually high yields.

[Main narrator, slower]

He also remembered another piece of arithmetic. Back at Texas Instruments, he had noticed a pattern: custom-made products earned far higher margins than standardized ones. Because custom work could not be copied, and its price could not be undercut.

And the foundry was, at bottom, a business of custom work from start to finish.

[Main narrator]

So he placed his bet: since building a fab was getting more and more expensive, one day the world would be full of thousands of companies that designed chips and owned no factory.

And TSMC would be the one shared factory for all of them.

That bet, as history would prove, was astonishingly accurate.

---

## Act Two · The Hard Beginning

[Main narrator]

The ideal was grand, but the reality was thin.

In its first years, TSMC's days were not easy.

Its first production line was an old line repurposed from ITRI. Its technology was still the seven-micron process bought from RCA years earlier — and although ITRI had pushed it from seven microns to five, then to four and a half, the rest of the industry had run faster, and TSMC had actually fallen further behind the cutting edge.

[Main narrator, slower]

Worse still, it did not even have a decent customer to its name.

Chang knew in his heart that TSMC's real future lay in the American and European markets. But back then, which big Western company would take a new Taiwanese fab seriously?

So he brought in a veteran of the American semiconductor world — Jim Dykes, the former head of General Electric's semiconductor business — to run operations and help open the door to the West.

[Main narrator, slower]

Dykes brought with him connections and credibility across the Atlantic and the Pacific. But credibility was one thing — the big companies were still watching from a distance, asking themselves whether this small Taiwanese factory could really deliver.

What TSMC needed was one chance to prove itself.

[Main narrator]

TSMC's very first customers were small design houses in Taiwan. The orders were scattered, and the margins were painfully thin.

[Main narrator, slower]

In those years, Chang poured nearly all his energy into that one old production line. He kept the word "yield" always on his lips, watching the numbers every day — to him, yield was the lifeblood of semiconductors. Every extra point of yield meant a notch lower cost; every notch lower cost meant one more order snatched from a rival.

He knew it himself: TSMC could not match the giants in technology or capital. The one thing it could offer was this obsession — pushing yield to the very limit.

[Main narrator]

But Chang did not panic. Because in his hands he held two things that others dismissed.

[Main narrator, slower]

The first was yield. He treated yield as an article of faith, repeating it since his ITRI days — on the same line, every extra point of yield meant one notch lower cost. And Taiwan's factories had an almost inexplicable gift for exactly this.

The second was the learning curve. Chang transplanted, wholesale, the playbook he had polished at Texas Instruments: push volume high enough, and costs march downward; lower costs mean lower prices; lower prices mean more and more orders.

[Main narrator]

It was a snowballing loop. And TSMC, very carefully, was setting it in motion.

---

## Act Three · We Never Compete with Our Customers

[Main narrator]

What truly opened the door for TSMC was a promise.

From the very first day, Chang had written it into TSMC's genes: **we design nothing, and we compete with no customer.**

[Main narrator, slower]

To an outsider, the sentence sounds ordinary. Inside the semiconductor world, it was worth its weight in gold.

Because in those days, asking another company to make your chips was a nerve-racking affair. The giants who owned fabs usually designed chips too — hand them your design, and you were handing your lifeblood to a potential rival. They might make your chip today and copy your design tomorrow; or they might prioritize their own products and leave you waiting.

Chang knew this fear all too well. He remembered that Texas Instruments had once been sued, accused of unfairly giving priority to its own products.

[Main narrator]

TSMC was different.

It had no chips of its own, and no products of its own, so it had no reason whatsoever to compete with its customers. Handing a design to TSMC was like handing cloth to a tailor — the tailor's only job is to make the clothes well, never to wear them and take your market.

On the strength of this one promise — "we never compete with our customers" — TSMC slowly knocked open one door after another.

[Main narrator, slower]

And just then, on the other side of the ocean, a quiet revolution was taking place.

[Main narrator, slower]

And, by the way, these eight years of TSMC's birth happened to fall inside one of the most surging waves in the history of technology.

In 1981, IBM released the first personal computer. Before long, clone-makers like Compaq and Dell poured in, and the personal computer walked into millions of homes. By the 1990s, Microsoft's Windows and Intel's processors formed the famous "Wintel" alliance, and the demand for computers came down like an avalanche.

And every single computer carried dozens — sometimes hundreds — of chips inside it.

Demand for chips was exploding, even as the cost of building a fab was exploding too. For the first time, the twin brothers "design" and "manufacturing" felt the urge to live apart.

[Main narrator]

In 1984, Gordon Campbell founded Chips and Technologies — the very man who, back in New York, had told Chang "I don't need to build a fab." In 1984, Cirrus Logic was founded. In 1985, Qualcomm. In 1991, Broadcom. In 1993, a company called Nvidia was born in Silicon Valley.

These companies shared one trait: they designed chips, and owned no factory.

[Main narrator, slower]

Each had its own specialty: Qualcomm did wireless communications, Chips and Technologies did PC chipsets, Cirrus Logic did audio and graphics chips, Broadcom did communications chips, and Nvidia did graphics processors.

And what they all lacked was the same thing: a factory they could trust — one that would not compete with them for business.

[Main narrator]

People gave them a name — "fabless" semiconductor companies: no fabrication plant.

In an era dominated by giants, these fledgling companies had neither the money to build a fab nor the wish to be squeezed by the giants. What they needed most was a factory they could trust — one that would never steal their business.

[Main narrator, slower]

Because once you handed your design to a giant that owned its own fabs, you were putting your whole life into the hands of someone who might turn around and devour you.

And these small companies could not afford that bet.

[Main narrator]

And TSMC happened to be standing in exactly that spot, waiting for them to come.

---

## Act Four · The Model Works

[Main narrator]

From the early 1990s, things began to change.

One after another, fabless design companies began handing their orders to TSMC. They came to Chang with designs, and Chang gave them a factory they could rely on — high yield, low cost, and a vow of silence, never competing for their market.

[Main narrator, slower]

TSMC's capacity began to expand at a speed visible to the naked eye.

In 1990, its second fab, Fab 2, was completed — TSMC's first plant that was fully its own. In 1993 came Fab 3, Taiwan's first eight-inch wafer fab.

One new fab a year. At the time, that was an almost unthinkable pace.

[Main narrator]

And TSMC's confidence rested on an iron triangle: yield, cost, and service.

Yield made its chips cheaper to produce. Cost let it squeeze its rivals on price. And service — the promise never to compete with customers — persuaded customers to entrust their most precious designs to it.

Put the three together, and you had a moat that no one in that era could copy.

[Main narrator, slower]

Now the numbers began to speak for TSMC.

In 1994, TSMC's revenue reached roughly seven hundred and forty-four million dollars.

On September 5 of that same year, TSMC listed on the Taiwan Stock Exchange. Its ticker: 2330.

[Main narrator, slower]

Going public meant a great deal for TSMC. It meant that a company which had once needed the government and Philips to keep it alive could now draw on the capital markets and roll its capacity forward on its own.

With money in hand, TSMC built even faster.

[Main narrator]

By 1995 — barely eight years after it opened its doors — TSMC's annual revenue crossed one billion dollars.

The company the whole industry had laughed at had become the undisputed king of the foundry business. And the words "pure-play foundry" had gone from heresy to the new common sense of the entire industry.

[Main narrator, slower]

There is something telling here: around the same time TSMC burst onto the scene, other countries and regions had also hit on the idea of the foundry. But in the end, only TSMC ran all the way with it.

The same card, played two ways — one became a trump, the other a dud. The difference lay entirely in that promise: "we never compete with our customers."

[Main narrator]

And there was something even more profound: TSMC's success, in turn, gave birth to a brand-new species.

Because of "shared factories" like TSMC, a team of just a few dozen people could now design a chip. They no longer needed a multi-billion-dollar fab — they only needed a design.

The barrier to designing chips had been torn down, overnight.

[Main narrator, slower]

It was something almost unheard of in the history of industry: suddenly, a whole field no longer demanded an astronomical entry ticket.

And TSMC was the one who tore up that ticket.

## Epilogue · A Parallel View

[Main narrator, quiet, slower]

Let us look at this story once more, from the beginning.

In 1987, a fifty-six-year-old man founded a company on a small island that nobody believed in.

It designed no chips, made no products of its own, and devoted itself to one thing — making other people's chips.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

Back then, that was heresy. But to Chang, it was the most natural thing in the world.

Because he had understood something deeper than "making chips":

In this industry, the scarcest resource is not technology, and not capital. It is **trust**.

[Main narrator, quiet]

The giants wanted to do everything themselves, so they did nothing perfectly, and they were forever watching their backs. TSMC, on the other hand, gave up its ambition to be the hero, and chose to step backstage and serve everyone else.

And in the end, it became the one everyone could not do without.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

We like to say the winner is the one who steps onto the podium.

But TSMC's story teaches us something else: **the disruptor does not have to play the hero. Build the platform — and let everyone else become the hero — and you become the greatest winner of all.**

[Main narrator, quiet]

Chang's promise, "we never compete with our customers," sounded humble. But it was precisely this humility that let Qualcomm, Broadcom, Nvidia, and countless later fabless companies entrust their futures to him.

He made no chips of his own — yet the whole world's chips now bear the mark of his foundry.

[Main narrator, with feeling]

A child who had walked out of the fires of war in the East, who had learned everything about semiconductors in the West, came back to the East at last and — with a model nobody believed in — connected the two worlds again.

That, perhaps, is the most moving face of the business spirit: it asks no questions about where you came from. It only asks whether you dare to do what everyone else is afraid to do.

[Main narrator, quiet]

Many years later, when people look back over half a century of semiconductors, they see one clear dividing line:

Before TSMC, a chip company had to both design and manufacture. After TSMC, design and manufacturing each became a vast world of their own.

And that line was drawn not by a technological breakthrough, but by the birth of a business model — a model that everyone laughed at, and that went on to change the world.

**He made no chips of his own — he only made other people's chips, and in doing so he rewrote an entire industry. The disruptor does not play the hero; he builds the platform, and lets everyone else become the hero.**

## Knowledge Card · Historical Background of This Episode

(Verified public facts, for the listener's reference)

1. **TSMC's Start**: Fab 1 (a repurposed ITRI line) came online in 1987; its first president was Jim Dykes, former head of GE's semiconductor business, who joined in early 1987; Fab 2 (its first fully owned plant) followed in 1990, and Fab 3 (Taiwan's first 8-inch fab) in 1993.
2. **A Backward Start**: ITRI pushed its process from 7 microns to 5, then 4.5 microns, but the industry moved faster, so TSMC began even further from the cutting edge; its greatest advantage was exceptionally high yields.
3. **The Pure-Play Model and "No Competition with Customers"**: TSMC designs no chips, sells no products of its own, and competes with no customer — it only manufactures. This promise was the key to winning fabless customers' trust.
4. **The Fabless Wave**: Qualcomm (1985), Chips and Technologies (1984), Cirrus Logic (1984), Broadcom (1991), Nvidia (1993), and Marvell (1995) — fabless design houses that became the natural customers of the pure-play model.
5. **TSMC Goes Public**: On September 5, 1994, TSMC listed on the Taiwan Stock Exchange under ticker 2330.
6. **Revenue Milestones**: 1994 revenue was about $744 million; in 1995 revenue crossed $1 billion (about $1.08 billion); 1996 revenue reached NT$39.4 billion (about $1.45 billion).
7. **The Learning Curve, Continued**: Chang carried the learning-curve playbook he had honed at Texas Instruments over to TSMC — using high yields and steady price cuts to roll a snowball of cost and orders.
